    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Employer of Record in the Philippines: Is It Legal - and Who Bears the Risk?</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on October 22, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">More foreign companies are hiring Filipino talent these days, and many of them do it through an Employer of Record, or EOR. It lets them hire here without setting up their own Philippine company first. Sounds convenient, right? It can be. But calling a company an "EOR" does not automatically make the arrangement legal.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">When a dispute comes up, Philippine law does not care much about labels. It looks at what is really happening: Who employs the worker? Who pays? Who controls the work? Who disciplines or dismisses the employee? Let us walk through what that means for both the foreign client and the EOR.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Is an Employer of Record?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In a typical setup, a Philippine company becomes the worker's official employer. It signs the employment contract, runs the payroll, withholds and pays the taxes, and takes care of SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG contributions. Meanwhile, the worker does the actual work for a foreign client.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The client usually decides what needs to get done, sets the goals and deadlines, and checks whether the work meets its business needs. That is a workable model. The legal question is whether the parties are really following it.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">There is No Special "EOR Law"</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Here is something many people do not realize: the Philippines has no law that creates a special category called an "Employer of Record." There is no EOR Law that says it is allowed, and none that says it is not.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">So an EOR arrangement is examined under our existing labor rules, especially the rules on contracting. DOLE Department Order No. 174, series of 2017, regulates these arrangements and strictly prohibits what is called "labor-only contracting."</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">A legitimate contractor needs more than a contract and a registration certificate. It has to run as a real, independent business, with enough capital of its own, genuine control over its employees' work, and compliance with labor standards. Registration and paperwork matter, but they do not settle the question by themselves. What the parties do day to day matters just as much.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Big Question: Who Controls the Work?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">There is a difference between telling a worker what result you need and controlling how the worker does the job.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A foreign client can reasonably set business goals, deadlines, quality standards and deliverables. But if the client's managers start acting like the employer, for example by deciding who gets hired, imposing discipline, approving leave, dictating how the work is done, or firing people directly, then the arrangement deserves a much closer look.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">A well-written agreement helps. But a labor tribunal would not simply accept what the contract says. It will look at what actually happened.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">When Does It Become Risky?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The biggest concern is labor-only contracting. In simple terms, this is when the middle company is little more than a name on paper. The usual warning signs are a contractor without real capital of its own whose workers do work directly related to the client's main business, or a contractor that does not truly control how its employees do their work.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The consequences can be serious. The client may be treated as the direct employer of the workers and face the liability that comes with it.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">That is why an EOR should not simply be a company that "puts people on payroll" while the foreign client acts as the employer in everything but name. The more the setup looks like a supply of workers under the client's control, the bigger the legal risk.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">"But the Client Is Based Abroad. Doesn't That Matter?"</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Less than you might think. If the work is performed in the Philippines, Philippine labor rules can still apply, even if the client's headquarters are overseas. A clause saying that foreign law applies, or that disputes must be settled in a foreign court, cannot simply wipe out a worker's mandatory rights.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The client's distance does create real practical headaches, such as enforcement, collection, taxes and corporate presence. But those are separate from the worker's basic employment rights.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Main Risks for the Foreign Client</h4>

        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Shared responsibility for unpaid wages.</strong> Even when the EOR is a legitimate contractor, the law can make the client answerable together with the EOR for unpaid wages and benefits. If the arrangement is treated as labor-only contracting, the exposure is even greater.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Being treated as the real employer.</strong> If the client hires, directs, disciplines or dismisses the workers itself, the law may treat it as their employer.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>An unreliable EOR.</strong> If the EOR does not pay wages or remit contributions properly, the workers' rights do not disappear. The client can end up facing contractual, legal and reputational problems.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Tax and company-registration questions.</strong> Having people work in the Philippines may raise Philippine tax and registration issues for the foreign business. These are worth checking separately with a tax or corporate adviser.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Data and ownership.</strong> Who owns the work product, and how employee and company information is handled across borders, should be spelled out clearly.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Main Risks for the EOR</h4>

        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>It carries the employer's responsibilities.</strong> If the EOR is the official employer, it can face claims about wages, benefits, discipline, dismissal and other employee rights.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The client does not pay.</strong> A payment dispute with the client does not cancel the employee's right to be paid. The EOR may still have to meet its obligations to the worker while chasing the client separately.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The client wants an employee gone.</strong> More on this below, because it trips up many EORs.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Employment status.</strong> Calling a worker probationary, fixed-term or project-based does not settle their real status. The law and the actual facts decide.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Collecting from overseas.</strong> If the client has no Philippine office or assets, enforcing its payment obligations can be slow and costly.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">When the Client Says, "We Don't Want This Employee Anymore"</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This is one of the most important things to settle before the arrangement even starts.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Imagine the foreign client tells the EOR: "We no longer want this employee. Please terminate the employment." That instruction, by itself, is not a legal ground for dismissal.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The EOR still has to ask: Is there a valid reason for dismissal under Philippine law? Were the required steps followed? Is separation pay or any other amount due? And if the client simply ends a project or its contract with the EOR, that does not automatically mean the employee can be let go. The deal between the client and the EOR is related to the employment, but it is not the same legal question.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">So a good EOR agreement should say, ahead of time, what happens when the client no longer wants someone assigned to it. That includes options such as reassignment, how much notice is needed, who funds the employee's costs in the meantime, and how the parties will decide whether a lawful termination is even available.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Why an "Indemnity Clause" Is not Enough</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An indemnity clause, where one side promises to cover the other's losses, is useful. It helps decide who pays between the EOR and the client when something goes wrong.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">But it is not a shield against labor law. It does not erase what employees are owed, and it does not stop a government agency or tribunal from holding someone liable when the law says so.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The better approach is to build real protections into the relationship from day one. Depending on the deal, that could include paying for payroll in advance, a security deposit or reserve for employment costs, the right to check records, proof that contributions and taxes were actually remitted, insurance, a guarantee from a parent company, clear payment deadlines, and the right to pause services after proper notice if the client does not pay.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Also take a careful look at any foreign arbitration or governing-law clause. It may help settle disputes between the two companies, but it should not be treated as a way to contract out of mandatory Philippine employment rights.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What the Foreign Client Should Ask For</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Before signing, go beyond "Are you registered?" Here are better questions:</p>

        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>How is the arrangement structured?</strong> Ask the provider to explain how it fits within Philippine employment and contracting rules.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Is the provider properly registered?</strong> Check its company registration, DOLE registration where required, permits and other credentials.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Who really acts as the employer?</strong> Clarify who handles hiring, payroll, leave, discipline, performance reviews and termination.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Where does the client's control stop?</strong> Agree on what your managers may direct and what stays with the EOR.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Are wages, taxes and contributions actually being paid?</strong> Ask for regular proof, such as payslips and remittance receipts.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>What happens if you stop using an employee?</strong> Settle reassignment, notice, funding and termination steps in advance.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>What financial protection is in place?</strong> Consider indemnity, insurance, guarantees and sensible payroll-funding arrangements.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>What if the EOR fails?</strong> Plan for audit rights, cure periods, emergency payroll, transition help, and, where the law allows, an orderly transfer of the employees.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Keep the Roles Clear in Real Life</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A good agreement is important, but both sides have to follow it in practice.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The client can set goals, deadlines, quality standards and deliverables. The EOR, for its part, has to genuinely carry out the employer's role it has promised to perform. It cannot just be a name on the contract and the payslip.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">One simple safeguard is a short, written "direction protocol" that tells the client's managers what they can and cannot do. It can cover day-to-day instructions, feedback, leave requests, disciplinary concerns, investigations, termination requests and how to communicate with the employees.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The point is not to stop the client from managing its business. It is to avoid a situation where the paperwork says one thing and the actual practice says another.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Bottom Line</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An EOR can be a practical way for a foreign business to work with Filipino talent, but the label is not a legal shield.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">What counts is not whose name is on the contract or the payslip. Philippine law looks at who truly employs the worker, who controls the work, whether the middle company is a genuine independent business, and whether the whole arrangement follows labor standards and the contracting rules.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If you are a foreign client, do not choose an EOR on price alone, or because someone promises, "we take care of everything." Check the provider, understand the structure, set clear boundaries, and plan for the day things do not go as expected.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">If you are an EOR, being the employer on paper means a lot more than running payroll. It means taking the legal responsibilities seriously, and making sure your agreement with the foreign client gives you the authority, information and financial protection to do the job properly.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;"><strong>Before using an EOR, or offering EOR services in the Philippines, have the structure, the service agreement and the employment documents reviewed under Philippine labor law.</strong></p>
