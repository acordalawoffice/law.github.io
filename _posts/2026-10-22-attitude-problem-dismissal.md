    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">"Bad Attitude" at Work: When Can an Employer Legally Dismiss an Employee?</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on October 22, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>
    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">"She just has a bad attitude."</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">You may have heard this from a supervisor who is tired of dealing with an employee who argues with colleagues, responds rudely to instructions, or seems to make every workplace interaction difficult.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Sometimes there is a genuine problem that needs to be addressed. But sometimes "bad attitude" is simply a label for someone who is difficult to work with, disagrees with management, or does not get along with the team.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The distinction matters. Philippine labor law does not recognize "bad attitude" as a separate ground for dismissal. Yet the Supreme Court has recognized that sufficiently serious, proven behavior associated with an attitude problem may justify termination under the appropriate legal ground.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">So where does the law draw the line? The answer begins with a simple question: what did the employee actually do?</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Start With the Conduct, Not the Label</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Article 297 of the Labor Code identifies the just causes for dismissal, including serious misconduct, willful disobedience, gross and habitual neglect of duties, fraud or willful breach of trust, and other causes analogous to those expressly listed.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">"Bad attitude" does not appear on that list. But the absence of that phrase does not mean an employee's behavior can never justify dismissal.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Consider the difference between an employee who occasionally disagrees with a supervisor and one who deliberately refuses a lawful and reasonable instruction. Or between someone who is not particularly sociable and someone who repeatedly threatens colleagues, seriously disrupts work, or commits established acts of misconduct.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">These situations should not be treated alike. Depending on the facts, the conduct may fall under a recognized just cause, provided the employer establishes the required elements of that ground.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The practical lesson for employers is straightforward: identify the actual behavior first. Do not begin with the conclusion that an employee has a bad attitude and then look for a legal ground to support it.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What the Supreme Court Has Said</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In <em>Heavylift Manila, Inc. v. Court of Appeals</em> (G.R. No. 154410, October 20, 2005), an employee's low performance rating and negative feedback from teammates about her "work attitude" were raised in a letter, and she was dismissed roughly six months later for loss of confidence.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court held that an employee's attitude problem can be a valid ground for dismissal, describing it as a situation analogous to loss of trust and confidence that the employer must duly prove. The ruling acknowledged a real workplace concern: an employee who cannot get along with colleagues can strain the working environment, and management has the prerogative to act to protect the organization.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">But recognizing that possibility is not the same as approving every dismissal based on an alleged attitude problem.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The Court still found the dismissal illegal. The employer had waited months before terminating the employee, apparently to give her a chance to improve — but that did not cure the real defect: the letter raising the concern never specified what she had actually done, never gave her a chance to explain, and never functioned as a valid first notice. General complaints and one letter referencing her attitude did not establish the allegations, and her silence in response was not treated as an admission. Good intentions and elapsed time do not substitute for specific facts and a fair process.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Eight Notices Do Not Automatically Make a Strong Case</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The lesson becomes even clearer in <em>Tay v. Apex 8 Studios, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 241360, July 6, 2021).</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In that case, the employer issued the employee eight notices to explain over a relatively short period. The allegations included inappropriate language, an allegedly aggressive manner, absence-related issues, and other accusations. The employer also relied on the employee's supposed inability to get along with other people.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The number of notices might suggest a substantial disciplinary record. But the Supreme Court examined what the notices and supporting evidence actually established.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Some allegations were expressed in general terms without sufficiently identifying the words or acts complained of. In one instance, conduct described as aggressive was not supported by the account of the actual exchange. Other allegations suffered from evidentiary weaknesses, including reliance on material that did not adequately establish the charges.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court did not treat the accumulation of accusations as a substitute for proof. The dismissal could not be sustained merely because the employer had repeatedly issued notices or characterized the employee's behavior negatively.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The decision also discussed <em>Sy v. Neat, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 213748, November 27, 2017). That case involved an employee with a longer disciplinary record covering several unrelated infractions, and the Court went through each one separately. The attitude-problem charge was one piece of that record, and it was the one the Court rejected outright: the employer's only support was an HR memo summarizing secondhand complaints from managers, with no incident reports and no performance evaluations behind it. The rest of the record did not save that particular charge — each allegation had to stand on its own evidence.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Should an Employer Document?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If an employee's behavior is affecting the workplace, the employer should move beyond general descriptions and record the incidents that give rise to the concern. Instead of writing "the employee has a negative attitude toward management," identify the specific incident: what instruction was given, how the employee responded, when it happened, and who directly witnessed the exchange.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Depending on the nature of the complaint, useful records may include:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 20px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Specific, dated incident reports describing what happened;</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Statements from witnesses with personal knowledge of the incident;</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Relevant written instructions, communications, or workplace records;</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Applicable company rules and evidence that the employee was informed of them;</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Previous coaching, warnings, or evaluations, when relevant to the alleged pattern of conduct; and</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">The employee's explanation and the employer's assessment of the evidence.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Not every case requires all these documents. What matters is that the employer can substantiate the allegations through substantial evidence — not proof beyond reasonable doubt, but relevant evidence a reasonable mind could accept as adequate to support the conclusion reached.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Does the Behavior Have to Violate a Company Policy?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A clear code of conduct can help. Rules on workplace respect, harassment, threats, insubordination, and professional behavior tell employees what the organization expects and help supervisors respond consistently when problems arise.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">But a company policy is not a shortcut to dismissal. A policy violation does not automatically establish serious misconduct, willful disobedience, or another just cause — the employer must still prove the relevant acts and satisfy the legal requirements of the ground invoked.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The same caution applies to analogous causes under Article 297. An employer cannot make any undesirable behavior a valid ground for dismissal simply by placing it in a handbook or calling it analogous to a statutory just cause. The conduct must genuinely qualify under the applicable legal standards.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Even Proven Misconduct Does Not Automatically Mean Dismissal</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Suppose an employee really did speak disrespectfully to a supervisor or behave inappropriately toward a colleague. That establishes a reason to address the conduct, but the next question is whether dismissal is legally justified. The nature and seriousness of the act, the circumstances in which it occurred, the employee's responsibilities, and the applicable rules all matter. A minor disagreement should not automatically be treated like a serious act of insubordination or misconduct.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The employer should also distinguish between a performance issue, a disciplinary offense, and conduct that satisfies a statutory ground for dismissal. These may overlap, but they are not interchangeable — and a penalty written into company policy does not, by itself, settle whether dismissal is the legally appropriate response.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Do Not Overlook Due Process</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Even when the employer believes it has a valid ground, the dismissal process matters. The employee must generally receive a first written notice identifying the specific ground and the facts supporting the charge, and a reasonable opportunity to explain — generally at least five calendar days from receipt of that notice. The employer must genuinely consider the employee's explanation before deciding, and if dismissal is warranted, a second written notice must communicate that decision.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">In <em>Agabon v. NLRC</em> (G.R. No. 158693, November 17, 2004), the Supreme Court held that an employer may still be liable for nominal damages when a valid substantive ground for dismissal exists but procedural due process was not observed. The reverse is equally true: following the notice procedure does not cure the absence of a valid ground. Both elements are required.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What If the Employee Holds a Position of Trust?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Employers sometimes invoke loss of trust and confidence when an employee's behavior damages the working relationship. But this ground has specific legal requirements — it is not a general remedy for a strained relationship or a supervisor's loss of patience.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">A managerial title alone does not establish a valid loss of trust, nor does a general allegation that an employee is difficult to work with. The reason must be grounded in proven conduct that meets the applicable legal standard.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A Practical Check Before Making a Decision</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If you are an employer or HR manager dealing with an employee whose behavior has become a recurring concern, pause before deciding that dismissal is the answer. Ask:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 20px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Can we identify the specific acts or omissions that caused the problem?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Can we establish those acts through reliable evidence?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">What legal ground, if any, do the proven facts support?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Have we considered the employee's explanation and the circumstances?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Is dismissal justified, or would a proportionate corrective measure be more appropriate?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Have we complied with the applicable procedural requirements?</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Where the concern is a continuing pattern of behavior rather than a serious, isolated incident, timely coaching, clear expectations, and consistent documentation can help address the problem before it escalates — and help the employer make better-informed decisions if dismissal eventually becomes necessary.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What If You Are the Employee?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Being blunt, unpopular, or unwilling to agree with every management decision does not automatically justify dismissal. If you are accused of having a bad attitude, ask what specific behavior is being questioned, read the notice carefully, and respond to the actual allegations with relevant facts.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">At the same time, disagreement is not a license to threaten colleagues, deliberately disobey lawful instructions, or otherwise violate workplace rules. The important distinction is between a personality conflict and behavior the law recognizes as a valid ground for disciplinary action.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Bottom Line</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Can an employee be dismissed for a bad attitude? The answer is yes, in appropriate circumstances — but not simply because management uses that label.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The employer must establish what actually happened, identify a valid legal ground, support the allegations with substantial evidence, determine whether dismissal is justified, and observe procedural due process. As the Supreme Court's decisions illustrate, even six months of patience or a stack of eight notices cannot substitute for proof.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">In the end, the law does not require employers to tolerate every kind of behavior. It does, however, require them to distinguish between an employee they find difficult and an employee whose proven conduct provides a lawful basis for dismissal.</p>
