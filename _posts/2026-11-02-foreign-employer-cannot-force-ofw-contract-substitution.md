    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Can a Foreign Employer Force an OFW to Sign a New Contract, Before or After Deployment?</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on November 2, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Imagine this: you have signed your employment contract, the Department of Migrant Workers (DMW) has approved it, and your flight is just around the corner. Then your foreign employer or agency sends you a new contract and tells you to sign it instead. The pay is lower, or the benefits are different. What do you do?</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Now imagine a different version. You have been working abroad for a few months when your employer suddenly says, "Please sign this new contract." Can they do that?</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">In both situations, the short answer to "Can they force me?" is no. An employer or agency cannot lawfully replace your approved contract with one that leaves you worse off without the required government approval, and even an approved change must still respect the minimum legal protections for workers. Pushing you to sign such a contract can land them in serious legal trouble. Whether a particular new paper crosses that line depends on what it changes, so do not assume it is "just a formality." Look at what has changed before you agree to anything.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Why the approved contract matters</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A DMW-approved contract is not just paperwork. Before it is approved, the government reviews it to make sure it meets the minimum standards that protect Filipino workers abroad, such as fair pay, decent working conditions, and proper benefits. That approved contract is the one the government has accepted as the basis of your deployment, and it is your starting point for what you were promised.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What the law says</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Section 6(i) of the Migrant Workers and Overseas Filipinos Act (Republic Act No. 8042, as amended by Republic Act No. 10022) treats it as illegal recruitment to substitute or alter, to the worker's disadvantage, an approved employment contract without the government's approval. The law protects the contract from the moment the parties sign it until it expires. That covers the time before you leave the Philippines, and it also covers the whole time you are working abroad.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Before the DMW took over, this work belonged to the POEA, and the court cases mentioned below involved POEA-approved contracts. The rule is the same.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court has also rejected the argument that an agency necessarily escapes liability simply because the worker refused to sign. In <em>Fil-Expat Placement Agency, Inc. v. Lee</em> (G.R. No. 250439, September 22, 2020), a dental specialist working in Saudi Arabia was repeatedly pressured by her employer to sign a new contract, even though she already had an approved one. She kept refusing. The agency argued that nothing wrong happened because she never signed. The Supreme Court disagreed. Relying on an earlier ruling, it recognized that an unsuccessful attempt to replace an approved contract can still lead to liability, and that a worker's refusal to sign does not automatically let the agency off the hook. Of course, whether a violation is actually proven still depends on the facts, such as what the new contract says and what the employer was trying to do.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What if it happens after you are already abroad?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The <em>Fil-Expat</em> case is a good example. The worker had already been working in Saudi Arabia for about five months when the trouble began. At one point she was also asked to sign a document written in Arabic, under which only half of her stipulated salary would be declared to the Saudi government. She was hesitant but eventually signed it, using a different signature. Her employer then repeatedly pressured her to sign a new employment contract. When she refused, she was given extra duties, threatened with a salary deduction, and told to move out of her accommodation. She asked the Philippine Embassy and Consulate for help and was eventually sent home.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Back in the Philippines, she filed a complaint. The Labor Arbiter ruled in her favor, the NLRC reversed that, and the Court of Appeals then restored the Labor Arbiter's decision. The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">It is important to see that the Court dealt with two separate questions. The first was whether the employer attempted to replace her approved contract. The Court upheld the finding that there was substantial evidence of that attempt, even though she refused to sign the new contract. The second was whether she had been forced out of her job, which the law calls constructive dismissal. The Court found that she had, but this finding rested on everything that happened after she refused: the verbal intimidation, the threats, and the treatment that made staying unbearable. In other words, a request to sign a new contract does not by itself mean you were dismissed. What mattered in this case was the pattern of mistreatment on top of the attempt to replace the contract. The attempt can still be a problem in its own right, even if nobody forces you out of your job.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The money awards that the Labor Arbiter originally ordered, and that the Court of Appeals restored, included her salary for the unfinished part of her contract, a refund of her placement fee, and damages. The lesson for workers already abroad is that your approved contract does not stop mattering once you start work, and holding your ground does not cost you your rights.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">"It is just a formality"</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Employers sometimes say the new contract is only for "uniformity," or that the local government requires it. The employer in the <em>Fil-Expat</em> case gave exactly those reasons, and an earlier body even accepted them. The Supreme Court did not. It found it hard to believe that a second contract was needed when the approved one already contained the agreed terms, and no foreign law requiring another contract was ever shown.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The laws of the host country can matter too, and some employers do have real paperwork to file. But an employer who says a foreign law requires another contract should be able to tell you which law, and should be able to show that the new paper does not cut your pay or benefits. If they cannot, treat it with caution.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Can changes be added later?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A change that would leave you worse off needs the government's approval first. In <em>Corpuz v. Gerwil Crewing Phils., Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 205725, January 18, 2021), the Supreme Court stressed a recruitment agency's continuing responsibility toward the workers it deploys, and noted that a licensed agency knows alterations to an approved contract require prior approval. It held the agency liable for damages when the worker's actual employer, ship, and job all turned out to be different from what was approved. A replacement paper does not become valid just because someone asked you to sign it.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Who can be held responsible?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The responsibilities of a recruitment agency in the Philippines do not end when you leave the country. In the <em>Corpuz</em> case, a seafarer's approved contract named one employer, one ship, and the job of Able Seaman. In reality, he ended up working for a different company, on a different ship, as an oiler. The agency said it had no idea what happened to him. The Supreme Court did not accept this. It said the agency could not feign ignorance, and ordered it to pay moral damages, exemplary damages, and attorney's fees.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Under Section 10 of the same law, the agency and the foreign employer are jointly and severally liable for the worker's money claims arising from the employment. In simple terms, once a covered liability is established, the worker can go after either one for the full amount of it. This liability continues for the whole contract, even if the contract is changed or replaced along the way, whether in the Philippines or abroad. What a worker actually recovers still depends on the law, the kind of claim, and the evidence.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Is any change ever allowed?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Not every change is automatically illegal. The law targets changes that are to the worker's disadvantage and made without government approval. A correction of an error, or a change that has been properly approved, is a different matter. But as the cases above show, explanations like "it is only a formality" get a close look. The practical rule is simple: if you are asked to sign something different from what was approved, stop and check before you sign.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What should you do if this happens to you?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">1. Do not sign a document you do not understand, especially one that changes your salary, benefits, duties, employer, or other important terms. Ask for a copy, ask for an explanation in a language you understand, and get guidance before agreeing. Refusing a worse contract is your right, whether you are still in the Philippines or already abroad.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">2. Keep copies of your approved contract, and of any new document you are asked to sign.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">3. Save the messages, emails, and chats where you were asked or pressured to sign.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">4. If you are abroad, reach out to your recruitment agency in the Philippines and to the Philippine Embassy or Consulate. Do not wait until you are back home to speak up.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">5. Report it to the DMW, and talk to a lawyer about your options. Depending on the facts, you may be able to file a complaint against the responsible parties and claim money damages or other relief. The right place to file and what you can recover depend on what happened and what you can prove.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Your approved contract is your shield. Hold on to it, and do not let anyone talk you into trading it for less.</p>
