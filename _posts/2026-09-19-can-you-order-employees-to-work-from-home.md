    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Can You Order Your Employees to Work from Home? What Philippine Law Says</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on September 19, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>The short answer: generally, no, not unless your employees agree to it.</strong> Under Philippine law, working from home (which the law calls <em>telecommuting</em>) is a voluntary arrangement. It works only when the employer and the employee both say yes. That agreement can already be written into an employment contract or a company policy the employee accepted. But an employer cannot simply announce a work-from-home setup and expect everyone to go along with it.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">That is the general rule. Emergencies, such as a calamity or a government-declared measure, may be covered by separate rules or advisories issued at the time, so it is always worth checking what applies before you act.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The law behind all this is <strong>Republic Act No. 11165, the Telecommuting Act</strong>, together with its revised implementing rules, <strong>DOLE Department Order No. 237, Series of 2022</strong>. These apply to private-sector employers that run a work-from-home program, including hybrid setups where employees split their time between home and the office.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Why Informal Arrangements Are Risky</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Many businesses started work-from-home through a group chat message or a verbal "okay," and simply kept going. The rules, however, expect evidence that both sides voluntarily agreed to the program. That evidence can be a separate policy, a clause in existing policies or employment contracts, or another convenient format. A loose understanding is hard to prove when a disagreement comes up.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">There is another reason to take this seriously. The rules also say that existing company practices or policies allowing work from home, with similar or better benefits, are to be respected. So a long-standing informal setup may already carry legal weight, and changing it deserves care.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Same Treatment, Wherever They Work</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Working from home does not make an employee any less an employee. The law says telecommuting employees must be treated the same as comparable employees at the office. In practice, that means:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 20px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Same pay and benefits.</strong> This includes overtime pay and night shift differential for authorized hours of work.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Same rest days and holidays,</strong> including regular holidays and special non-working days.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Same training, career growth, and performance reviews.</strong> Workload and performance standards should be comparable to those of on-site colleagues, unless both sides agree on different standards that make more sense for a home setup.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Same rights as everyone else,</strong> including the freedom to communicate with worker representatives.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Staying connected.</strong> Employers should take reasonable steps so remote staff do not become isolated, such as regular chances to meet colleagues in person or online.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Two points tend to catch employers off guard:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 20px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Working time counts, wherever it happens.</strong> All the time an employee is required to be on duty, and all the time you allow them to work, counts as hours worked. Late-night replies and after-hours tasks can add up to overtime.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>"They work from home, so no overtime" is not a safe assumption.</strong> Telecommuting employees are not considered "field personnel" (who are generally not entitled to overtime pay) unless their actual working hours truly cannot be determined with reasonable certainty.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Who Pays for the Equipment?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The rules treat the facilities, equipment, and supplies needed for work-from-home, including the cost of acquiring, using, maintaining, repairing, and returning them, as ordinary and necessary business costs of the employer. In plain terms, these are business expenses, and they belong to the employer. If you plan to give an allowance for things like internet or electricity, spell out the details in your policy.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Your WFH Policy Should Cover</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The rules require a telecommuting program to include provisions that make it work in practice, and the list they give is not exhaustive. At the very least, your policy should address:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 20px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Eligibility:</strong> which roles or employees qualify.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Where they may work:</strong> home, co-working spaces, or other approved locations.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Equipment and technology:</strong> what the company provides, and how it is cared for and returned.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Health and safety:</strong> practical guidance for a safe home setup, such as ergonomics and how to report a work-related injury.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Performance evaluation:</strong> how output is measured and reviewed.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Code of conduct:</strong> the workplace standards that still apply at home.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Data protection and confidentiality:</strong> safeguards for company and client information, in line with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173).</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Emergency plans:</strong> what to do during power outages, internet failures, or severe weather.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Duration:</strong> how long the arrangement lasts.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Settling disagreements:</strong> the steps to follow when a problem comes up.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">One more item is not on the official list but is well worth adding: <strong>working hours and how time is recorded.</strong> That is often where overtime disagreements start.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If a disagreement does arise, the rules point first to the grievance process in your policy. If there is none, the parties should try to settle it through dialogue and consultation. If it is still unresolved, they can ask the DOLE regional or field office for conciliation or mediation, or take it to voluntary arbitration.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Tell DOLE and Keep Your Records</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Employers must notify DOLE that they are implementing telecommuting through the <strong>Establishment Report System (ERS)</strong>. If any branches or satellite offices are running work-from-home, include them in the notice. Also keep the documents showing that you and your employees voluntarily adopted the arrangement for <strong>at least three years</strong>.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Bottom Line</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">So, can you order your team to work from home? Not on your say-so alone. Get their agreement, put it in writing, treat remote employees the same as those in the office, and notify DOLE. A signed telecommuting agreement is not the only way to show consent, but it is the simplest, and it protects both sides. Handled well, work from home can be a win for everyone.</p>
