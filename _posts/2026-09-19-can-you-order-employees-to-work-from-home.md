    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Can You Order Your Employees to Work from Home? What Philippine Law Says</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on September 19, 2026 &bull; Updated on November 21, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Your business is shifting to a work-from-home arrangement. Perhaps you want to reduce office expenses, improve productivity, or keep operations running during difficult circumstances. Can you simply tell your employees to stay home and work remotely?</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Not necessarily. Under Philippine law, a telecommuting program is generally voluntary and requires the mutual agreement of the employer and the employee.</strong> An employer cannot simply impose a telecommuting program on employees without considering this requirement. The arrangement may already be covered by an employment contract, an existing company policy, or another agreement that the employee voluntarily accepted. What matters is that there is evidence that both sides agreed to adopt the program.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">There is an important distinction, however. The Telecommuting Act governs arrangements that allow employees to work from an alternative workplace. It does not automatically eliminate an employer's management prerogative to determine work assignments and work locations under an existing employment arrangement. Whether a particular workplace instruction falls within that authority depends on the employment contract, company policies, established practices, and the circumstances. Like other exercises of management prerogative, decisions must be made in good faith and must not result in an unlawful demotion, diminution of pay or benefits, or other impairment of employee rights.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The principal law governing telecommuting is <strong>Republic Act No. 11165, or the Telecommuting Act</strong>, together with its Revised Implementing Rules and Regulations under <strong>DOLE Department Order No. 237, Series of 2022</strong>. These rules apply to private-sector employers and employees implementing a telecommuting program, including hybrid arrangements where employees divide their working time between home and the office.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Exactly Is Telecommuting?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Working from home is only one form of telecommuting. Under the Revised IRR, telecommuting refers to a work arrangement that allows an employee to work from an alternative workplace, in whole or in part, using telecommunications or computer technologies.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An alternative workplace may be the employee's residence, a co-working space, or another location away from the employer's principal place of business where work can be performed using the necessary technology. This means that the law also covers hybrid arrangements and other approved remote-working setups.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Why Voluntary Agreement Matters</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Telecommuting Act allows an employer to offer a telecommuting program on a voluntary basis and upon terms and conditions mutually agreed upon by the parties. Employees may also propose such an arrangement to their employer.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The agreement does not necessarily have to be a separate document signed by every employee. Under Section 5 of the Revised IRR, the telecommuting program may be a separate policy, may be incorporated into existing policies or employment contracts, or may take another form convenient to the parties. In every case, however, there must be evidence that the employer and employees voluntarily agreed to adopt it.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Many businesses started work-from-home arrangements through group chat messages or verbal agreements and simply continued the practice. While these communications may help establish what the parties agreed upon, relying on informal arrangements can create problems when a disagreement arises. A written policy or agreement makes the terms clearer and provides better evidence of consent.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">There is another reason to take existing arrangements seriously. Section 11 of the Revised IRR provides that the terms of any similar voluntary agreement between an employer and an employee, such as an existing company practice or policy allowing work from home or similar arrangements with substantially similar or higher benefits, must be respected and continue to be effective, unless they do not conform with the Revised Rules.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This does not mean that every occasional instance of working from home automatically becomes a protected company practice. The arrangement must qualify under Section 11. Employers should examine how it was adopted, whether the parties voluntarily agreed to it, and what benefits and conditions it provides before deciding to change it.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Same Treatment, Wherever They Work</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Working from home does not make an employee any less an employee. Under Section 5 of RA 11165 and Section 8 of the Revised IRR, telecommuting employees must receive the same treatment as comparable employees working at the employer's regular workplace.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In practical terms, this means:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 20px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Same pay and monetary benefits.</strong> Telecommuting employees must receive a rate of pay, including overtime pay and night shift differential, and other similar monetary benefits not lower than those required by applicable laws or the collective bargaining agreement for authorized hours of work under the telecommuting agreement.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Same rest days and holidays.</strong> They retain their rights to rest days, regular holidays, and special non-working days.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Comparable workload and performance standards.</strong> Their workload and performance standards should be the same or equivalent to those of comparable employees working at the employer's premises. The parties may mutually agree on different performance standards that are more appropriate for the alternative workplace.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Equal access to training and career development.</strong> Remote employees must have the same access to training and career development opportunities as comparable employees, and must be subject to the same appraisal policies, without additional cost.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Appropriate technical training.</strong> Employers must provide appropriate training, without additional cost, on the technical equipment available to telecommuting employees and the characteristics and conditions of telecommuting.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Same collective rights.</strong> Employees working remotely retain their collective rights and must not be barred from communicating with their workers' representatives.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Employers must also take reasonable measures, as far as practicable, to prevent remote employees from becoming isolated from the rest of the working community. This may include giving them regular opportunities to meet colleagues, either physically or through telecommunications, and allowing access to company premises and information when practicable.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Two points about working hours and overtime deserve particular attention.</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 20px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Working time counts, wherever it happens.</strong> Under Section 4 of the Revised IRR, work performed in an alternative workplace is considered work performed in the regular workplace. All time an employee is required to be on duty, and all time the employer permits or allows the employee to work in the alternative workplace, must be counted as hours worked. If an employee performs compensable work beyond normal working hours with the employer's knowledge or permission, overtime liability may arise. Employers should establish clear rules on working hours, timekeeping, and after-hours communications.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Working from home does not automatically make an employee field personnel.</strong> Under the same Section 4, telecommuting employees are not considered field personnel unless their actual hours of work cannot be determined with reasonable certainty. An employer cannot avoid overtime obligations simply by classifying an employee as a remote worker.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In other words, an employee who works from home does not lose the right to overtime pay merely because the work is performed outside the office. The actual working arrangement and applicable labor standards remain important.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Who Pays for the Equipment and Other Work Expenses?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Section 9 of the Revised IRR treats the facilities, equipment, and supplies necessary to implement telecommuting and enable employees to perform their work in an alternative workplace as ordinary and necessary costs of the employer's business.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This includes expenses for acquiring, properly handling, using, maintaining, repairing, and returning the necessary equipment and supplies. In practical terms, the employer should identify what equipment and facilities the company will provide and how they will be maintained and returned.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">What about recurring expenses such as internet connectivity and electricity? The rules do not set a fixed amount or allowance for these. The employer should therefore address them clearly in the telecommuting agreement or policy, including any allowance, reimbursement, or other agreed arrangement. The policy should explain what resources the employee is expected to provide and which expenses the company will cover. Do not leave employees guessing about who pays for what.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Your WFH Policy Should Cover</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A good telecommuting policy should do more than announce that employees may work from home. Section 6 of the Revised IRR requires the program to contain provisions reasonably necessary or relevant to its effective implementation. The list is not exhaustive.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">At the very least, employers should address the following:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 20px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Eligibility:</strong> Which positions, functions, or employees qualify for telecommuting, considering job qualifications, the nature of the work, logistical requirements, personal circumstances, and performance.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Alternative workplaces:</strong> Where employees may work, such as their residence, approved co-working spaces, or other authorized locations.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Equipment and technology:</strong> The minimum computer hardware and software, internet connectivity and security, and other equipment and supplies needed to perform the work.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Occupational safety and health:</strong> Practical guidance on ergonomics, good housekeeping, lighting, sound, and temperature control, as well as mental health programs as reasonably necessary, to protect the physical and mental well-being of employees.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Performance evaluation:</strong> Common performance standards for telecommuting employees and comparable on-site employees, how performance is evaluated and monitored, how feedback is given, and immediate steps to address performance issues.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Code of conduct:</strong> Attendance, appearance and demeanor during virtual meetings, submission of accomplishment reports, and measures to ensure compliance.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Data protection, confidentiality, and security:</strong> Standards to protect personal information, sensitive personal information, and proprietary information, consistent with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173), its implementing rules, and National Privacy Commission issuances. Under Section 6 of the Telecommuting Act itself, the employer is responsible for taking appropriate data protection measures and for informing employees of the relevant laws and company rules, while the employee must keep confidential and proprietary information protected at all times.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Emergency protocols:</strong> Measures for device breakdowns, poor internet connection, power interruptions, weather disturbances, and similar events.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Duration and changes in the arrangement:</strong> The date the program takes effect and how long it will last, including termination or change of the arrangement due to an employee's request, business necessity, or change in operations, and a return to the previous work arrangement if the program is not renewed or extended.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Dispute resolution:</strong> A grievance machinery to resolve all grievances arising from the implementation and enforcement of the program, which must include an agreement for referral to voluntary arbitration.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">There are also employment conditions that must be included under Section 4 of RA 11165. The terms of the arrangement may not be less than the minimum labor standards set by law, and they must include:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 20px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Compensable work hours:</strong> Which working time will be counted and paid.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Minimum number of work hours:</strong> The minimum working hours required under the arrangement.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Overtime:</strong> The rules governing authorization, recording, and payment of overtime work.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Rest days:</strong> The employee's applicable rest-day schedule.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Leave benefits:</strong> The employee's entitlement to applicable leave benefits.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The same section adds an important requirement: in all cases, the employer must give the telecommuting employee relevant written information that adequately explains the terms and conditions of the program and the employee's responsibilities. Putting the arrangement in writing is therefore not only good practice. It is part of what the law expects.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Revised IRR also requires employers to preserve minimum labor standards and existing employment conditions. Social welfare benefits, security of tenure, and other rights under labor laws, company policies, and applicable agreements do not disappear simply because an employee works remotely.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Working hours and timekeeping are therefore not merely optional additions to a WFH policy. They are important components of the arrangement and should be clearly established from the beginning. This helps prevent disputes over attendance, overtime, and compensation.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Can You Require Employees to Return to the Office?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A work-from-home arrangement does not necessarily have to continue indefinitely. Under Section 6(i) of the Revised IRR, the telecommuting program should state its date of effectivity and duration, including termination or change of the work arrangement due to an employee's request, business necessity, or change in operations. It should also provide for a return to the previous work arrangement if the program is not renewed or extended.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">However, employers should not assume that they can unilaterally withdraw an established WFH arrangement without considering the terms of the agreement, applicable company policies, and existing employment conditions. Section 4 of the Revised IRR provides that telecommuting shall not in any way diminish or impair the terms and conditions of employment contained in any applicable company policy or practice, individual contract, or collective bargaining agreement.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The practical lesson is simple: establish clear rules on how the arrangement may be changed or ended before implementing it. If business operations later require employees to return to the office, review the agreement and the circumstances carefully before issuing instructions.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What About Emergencies?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Calamities, severe weather, public emergencies, and government restrictions may require businesses to reconsider where employees perform their work. However, neither the Telecommuting Act nor the Revised IRR contains a general emergency exception to the voluntary nature of telecommuting.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Employers should examine the specific government issuance or applicable regulation governing the situation rather than assume that an emergency automatically authorizes them to impose a WFH arrangement. The emergency protocols required under Section 6(h) of the Revised IRR address operational problems such as device breakdowns, poor internet connection, power interruptions, and weather disturbances. They should not be mistaken for a general exemption from the mutual-agreement requirement.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A clear policy, together with an understanding of the applicable emergency rules, helps employers respond without unnecessarily compromising employee rights.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">How Should Disagreements Be Resolved?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Under Section 9 of the Revised IRR, differences about the implementation or interpretation of a telecommuting program are treated as grievances. They should first be addressed through the grievance mechanism established under the program, company policy, or applicable collective bargaining agreement.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If there is no grievance mechanism, the parties should try to resolve their differences through dialogue and consultation. If the matter remains unresolved, either party may request assistance from the DOLE Regional or Field Office with jurisdiction over the regular or alternative workplace for conciliation or mediation under DOLE Department Order No. 151, Series of 2016.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The parties may also submit an unresolved grievance to voluntary arbitration. In fact, Section 6(j) of the Revised IRR requires the grievance machinery of every telecommuting program to include an agreement for referral to voluntary arbitration. Employers should therefore build this agreement into the policy from the start.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Notify DOLE and Keep Your Records</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Under Section 10 of the Revised IRR, employers implementing a telecommuting program must notify the DOLE through the <strong>Establishment Report System (ERS)</strong>. The notice must include all branches, satellite offices, or similar operational units implementing telecommuting.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Employers must also keep and maintain, as part of their records, the documents proving that the parties voluntarily adopted telecommuting for <strong>at least three years</strong>.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">These requirements are important. Proper documentation helps establish that the arrangement was voluntarily adopted and provides a reference when questions arise about its terms, implementation, or administration.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Bottom Line</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">So, can you order your employees to work from home? Under the Telecommuting Act, you generally cannot impose a telecommuting program without their voluntary agreement. The law recognizes telecommuting as an arrangement based on mutual consent, not simply a management instruction.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A separate signed telecommuting agreement is not the only way to establish consent. The arrangement may also be incorporated into an employment contract or company policy, provided there is evidence that the employer and employees voluntarily agreed to it. Whichever form you choose, make sure the terms are clear, explained to employees in writing, and properly documented.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Treat remote employees fairly, establish clear working-hour and compensation rules, provide the necessary equipment and support, protect company and employee information, and comply with DOLE notification and recordkeeping requirements. If you intend to change or end the arrangement later, review the agreement and existing employment conditions before acting.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A well-designed telecommuting program does more than allow employees to work outside the office. It gives the business flexibility while protecting the rights and interests of the people who keep it running.</p>
