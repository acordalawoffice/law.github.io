<h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Notice to Explain (NTE): What It Is and Why Employers Cannot Skip It</h3>
<div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on November 6, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>
<div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Imagine reporting for work one morning and being told: “You are terminated, effective today.” No warning. No chance to explain. Just a termination letter and a security guard waiting to walk you out.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Can an employer really do that? When an employee is being dismissed for a just cause, the answer is no. Philippine labor law requires the employer to give the employee written notice of the charges, and a real chance to answer them, before any final decision is made.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The document that starts this process is commonly called a <strong>Notice to Explain</strong>, or <strong>NTE</strong>. But a good NTE does not start with the letter. It starts with the work an employer does before writing it. Let us walk through all of it together.</p>
<h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">So, What Exactly Is a Notice to Explain?</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An NTE is a written notice that tells an employee which specific acts or omissions are being looked into, and asks the employee to explain why disciplinary action, including possible dismissal, should not be imposed.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">It is not a punishment, and it is not a finding that the employee is guilty. Think of it as the employer saying, “Here is what we have heard. Please tell us your side.” It gives the employee a chance to understand the accusation, present a defense, and share circumstances that management may not know about yet.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">That is why an employer should never issue an NTE just to create paperwork for a decision that has already been made. The whole point is to find out what really happened before deciding what, if anything, should be done.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An NTE is not limited to cases where dismissal is possible. Whether the alleged violation is minor or serious, an employee should be informed of the specific charge and given a meaningful opportunity to explain before the employer imposes a disciplinary penalty that affects employment, such as a suspension. This is part of the employee’s right to procedural due process. The Supreme Court has held that any disciplinary action affecting employment must satisfy both substantive and procedural due process (<em>Montinola v. Philippine Airlines</em>, G.R. No. 198656, September 8, 2014). In <em>Bohol Wisdom School v. Mabao</em> (G.R. No. 252124, July 23, 2024), the Court declared a teacher’s suspension illegal, finding that the alleged conduct was not a valid ground for the penalty and that the employer had violated procedural due process. The Court rejected the argument that the employee’s earlier discussion with school officials and her admission of the violation were enough, noting that no Notice to Explain had been issued before the suspension. Employers are also well advised to issue an NTE before a formal disciplinary warning, as in <em>Sta. Isabel v. Perla Compañia de Seguros, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 219430, November 7, 2016), where NTEs preceded both a verbal warning and a final written warning. The full two-notice procedure, ending in a notice of termination, applies when the employer is dismissing an employee for a just cause, and that is the focus of this article.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">A quick note on scope: the dismissals discussed here are those based on <em>just causes</em> under Article 297 of the Labor Code. Dismissals for <em>authorized causes</em>, such as redundancy, retrenchment, and closure, follow a different notice procedure, which we touch on near the end.</p>
<h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Law Requires It?</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An employee’s right to procedural due process requires that the employee be informed of the charge and given a meaningful opportunity to explain before disciplinary action affecting employment is imposed. An NTE is the written notice commonly used for this purpose. Several sources of law and jurisprudence recognize and implement this protection:</p>
<ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The Constitution.</strong> Article XIII, Section 3 recognizes workers’ right to security of tenure and full protection of their rights.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Article 292(b) of the Labor Code</strong> (formerly Article 277[b]) governs the procedure for termination. It requires the employer to give the employee a written notice stating the causes for termination, and to give the employee ample opportunity to be heard and to defend himself or herself.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Article 297 of the Labor Code</strong> (formerly Article 282) lists the just causes for which an employer may terminate employment.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>DOLE Department Order No. 147-15</strong> provides the implementing rules on termination and procedural due process.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Supreme Court decisions.</strong> <em>King of Kings Transport, Inc. v. Mamac</em> explains what the first notice must contain in dismissal cases, while <em>Montinola v. Philippine Airlines</em> and <em>Bohol Wisdom School v. Mabao</em> apply due process to disciplinary action short of dismissal.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The key point is that an employer needs <em>both</em> a valid ground for dismissal <em>and</em> compliance with the required procedure. Having one does not excuse the absence of the other.</p>
<h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Two-Notice Rule in Plain Words</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">When an employer is considering dismissal for a just cause, the process generally involves two written notices and a real opportunity to be heard:</p>
<ol style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The first notice (the NTE).</strong> It states the specific acts or omissions charged, the company rule involved (if any), and the legal ground being considered. It invites the employee to explain.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A chance to be heard.</strong> The employee must be allowed to submit an explanation, present evidence, and answer the accusation, in writing or in person. A formal hearing or conference becomes necessary in certain situations, which we discuss below.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The second notice (the decision).</strong> After weighing everything, including the employee’s explanation, the employer tells the employee in writing what it decided and why.</li>
</ol>
<p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">These steps are not just administrative boxes to tick. They exist so that no one loses a job without knowing the accusation and getting a fair chance to answer it.</p>
<h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Before the NTE: The Homework Employers Should Do First</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An NTE should not be the first thing an employer does after hearing a complaint. Before issuing one, management should be able to show there is a reasonable factual basis for asking the employee to explain.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This does not mean running a full investigation before sending the notice. It means doing enough preliminary work to identify the incident, understand the available evidence, and pinpoint exactly what needs to be explained.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Here are the practical steps a careful employer takes:</p>
<ol style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Document the incident.</strong> Get the incident report, written complaint, or supervisor’s report. Note the dates, times, places, persons involved, and circumstances.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Check the available evidence.</strong> Review CCTV footage, attendance records, emails, transaction records, receipts, memos, and witness accounts. The goal is a reasonable factual basis for the charge, not deciding guilt before hearing the employee’s side.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Identify the applicable rule and legal ground.</strong> Does the conduct violate a company policy, or fall under a just cause in Article 297? A written company rule is not required in every case, since some acts are just causes by law even without a handbook provision. But if the employer relies on a company policy, it should be able to show the policy was properly communicated to the employee.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Check that the charge fits the incident.</strong> Look at the nature of the act, the employee’s duties, and the surrounding circumstances. Identify what the employee is actually being asked to explain, and which company rule or Article 297 ground it falls under. A clear, accurate charge keeps the NTE fair and makes it easier for the employee to respond.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Decide whether preventive suspension is truly necessary.</strong> It is allowed only when the employee’s continued presence poses a serious and imminent threat to the life or property of the employer or co-workers. It is not a penalty. It generally cannot go beyond 30 days, and if it is extended, the employee must be reinstated or paid wages and benefits during the extension.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Assign someone to handle the case objectively.</strong> An HR officer or designated investigator should handle the process fairly. As a matter of good governance, avoid letting someone personally involved in the complaint run the investigation and decide the outcome.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Keep it confidential and document the service.</strong> Avoid unnecessary disclosures or premature remarks about the employee’s guilt. Serve the NTE personally whenever practicable, or at the employee’s last known address as the rules allow, and keep reliable proof of service or attempted service. If the employee refuses to receive it, write down the circumstances of the refusal.</li>
</ol>
<div style="background: #f7fafc; border-left: 4px solid #2b6cb0; padding: 15px 20px; margin: 20px 0; text-align: left;">
<p style="margin: 0 0 8px 0;"><strong>Before issuing an NTE, can the employer answer these questions?</strong></p>
<ul style="margin: 0; padding-left: 22px;">
<li style="margin-bottom: 5px;">Do we have a reasonable factual basis for the accusation?</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 5px;">Have we identified the specific conduct that needs to be explained?</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 5px;">Have we identified the company rule, if any, and the correct legal ground?</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 5px;">Does the charge we are about to make fit what actually happened?</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 0;">Are we ready to consider the employee’s explanation with an open mind?</li>
</ul>
</div>
<p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Once the groundwork is done, the employer can prepare the NTE. Its quality matters, because no one can defend against an accusation that has not been clearly explained.</p>
<h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What a Proper NTE Must Say</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In <em>King of Kings Transport, Inc. v. Mamac</em> (G.R. No. 166208, June 29, 2007), the Supreme Court explained that the first notice must contain a detailed narration of the facts and circumstances behind the charge. A general description is not enough.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A proper NTE should therefore include:</p>
<ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The specific acts or omissions.</strong> What the employee allegedly did or failed to do, with dates, times, places, transactions, persons involved, and other details that identify the incident.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The company rule or legal ground.</strong> The policy allegedly violated, if any, and the just cause under Article 297 being considered, so the employee understands the nature of the accusation.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A reasonable period to respond.</strong> Where dismissal is contemplated, at least five calendar days from receipt, so the employee can study the accusation, consult a lawyer or representative, gather evidence, and think through possible defenses.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>An opportunity to present a defense.</strong> A reminder that the employee may submit a written explanation, present supporting evidence, and ask for a hearing or conference.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The right to assistance.</strong> The employee may be assisted by a representative or counsel, if desired.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A neutral and respectful tone.</strong> The notice should present an accusation to be answered, not a conclusion that the employee has already been found guilty.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Here is how a weak NTE differs from a better one (both are sample texts for illustration):</strong></p>
<div style="background: #fffaf0; border-left: 4px solid #dd6b20; padding: 15px 20px; margin: 0 0 15px 0; text-align: left;">
<p style="margin: 0 0 6px 0;"><strong>Weak:</strong></p>
<p style="margin: 0; font-style: italic;">“You are hereby required to explain within 24 hours why you should not be terminated for your violation of company policy.”</p>
</div>
<div style="background: #f0fff4; border-left: 4px solid #38a169; padding: 15px 20px; margin: 0 0 20px 0; text-align: left;">
<p style="margin: 0 0 6px 0;"><strong>Better:</strong></p>
<p style="margin: 0; font-style: italic;">“It was reported that on October 10, 2026, at around 3:00 p.m., you were seen on CCTV taking two boxes of company inventory from Warehouse B without a release slip. This incident may constitute unauthorized removal of company property and may violate Section 4.2 of the Employee Code of Conduct. If proven, this conduct may constitute serious misconduct, a just cause for termination under Article 297(a) of the Labor Code. You are directed to submit your written explanation within five (5) calendar days from receipt of this notice, stating why disciplinary action should not be taken. You may submit supporting evidence, request a formal conference or hearing, and be assisted by a representative or counsel of your choice.”</p>
</div>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Notice what the second version does. It names the alleged conduct, the incident, the company rule involved, and the time given to respond. It also makes clear that the matter is still being looked into and that no final decision has been made.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">One more thing: the NTE should name the actual grounds being investigated. An employer should not charge an employee with one offense and then dismiss them for a completely different one they never had the chance to answer.</p>
<h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Is a Formal Hearing Always Necessary?</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Not always. This is a point many employers get confused about.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The law requires an <em>ample opportunity to be heard</em>. That means a meaningful chance, in writing or in person, to answer the charges and submit evidence. In <em>Perez v. Philippine Telegraph and Telephone Company</em> (G.R. No. 152048, April 7, 2009), the Supreme Court explained that an ample opportunity to be heard does not necessarily require a formal hearing. The Court restated these principles in <em>Reyes-Rayel v. Philippine Luen Thai Holdings Corporation</em> (G.R. No. 174893, July 11, 2012), where a managerial employee who answered the notice in writing was held to have been given due process, because she never asked for a hearing and no company policy required one. Under these decisions, a formal hearing or conference becomes mandatory when:</p>
<ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">The employee requests it in writing;</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">There are substantial disputes about the facts or evidence;</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Company rules or established practice require an actual hearing; or</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Similar circumstances justify one.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">So, for example, if an employee writes back, “I would like a conference to explain my side,” the employer should hold one.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">At the same time, employers should not assume that merely receiving a written explanation settles the due process requirement in every case. What matters is that the employee gets a genuine opportunity to explain, present evidence, and answer the accusations before management decides.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The employee may also ask for copies of records and other materials relevant to the defense. The employer should provide reasonable access to evidence that is material to the employee’s defense, subject to legitimate confidentiality limits.</p>
<h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Common NTE Mistakes</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Even employers who know the two-notice rule can stumble on details that weaken the whole process:</p>
<ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Vague charges.</strong> Words like “misconduct,” “insubordination,” or “violation of company policy,” without specific facts, leave the employee guessing.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Too little time.</strong> When dismissal for a just cause is contemplated, giving only 24 or 48 hours to answer falls short of the five-calendar-day standard.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>No stated ground.</strong> The notice should identify the company rule, if any, and/or the relevant just cause under the Labor Code.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Pre-judging the case.</strong> Statements like “you are guilty” or “your dismissal has already been decided” show that management has closed its mind.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Combining the NTE and the termination decision.</strong> The employee must be allowed to answer <em>before</em> the employer decides and announces the outcome.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Penalizing first and asking questions later.</strong> Imposing a suspension, or announcing a sanction, and only then asking the employee to explain defeats the purpose of the NTE. The employee must be heard before the employer decides.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Ignoring the employee’s explanation.</strong> Management must actually consider the response and supporting evidence, not just file it away.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Weak proof of service.</strong> The employer should be able to show that the notice was properly served, or that reasonable efforts were made to serve it.</li>
</ul>
<h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A Real Case: When 48 Hours Is Not Enough</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A good illustration is <em>Verizon Communications Philippines, Inc. v. Margin</em> (G.R. No. 216599, September 16, 2020). A network engineer fell seriously ill and told his supervisor by text message that he had been diagnosed with pulmonary tuberculosis and had to rest. After more than a month, the company sent a nurse to his house to serve an NTE, which gave him only 48 hours to explain. Since he was away, the notice was left with a cousin. He actually learned of it days later, called his supervisor, and emailed his explanation the same day. But the company ignored it because it was past the 48-hour deadline, and dismissed him.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court ruled that the employee was denied procedural due process. He was not given ample time to prepare his defense, and his explanation was set aside simply because it came after the deadline. The Court also found that his absence could not properly be treated as unauthorized, since he had notified his supervisor of his illness, and that dismissal was in any case too harsh a penalty under the circumstances.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The Court ordered separation pay in lieu of reinstatement. It did not award backwages, because the employee, while not at fault for being sick, had also done little else to keep the company informed, and the employer was found to have acted in good faith. The lesson for employers: a short deadline and a rigid reading of company rules can turn a disciplinary case into an illegal dismissal.</p>
<h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">If You Are the Employee Who Received an NTE</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">First, take a breath. Receiving an NTE does not mean you have been dismissed. It means your employer is asking you to answer an accusation, and that is your chance to be heard. Here are some practical things to keep in mind:</p>
<ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Read the notice carefully.</strong> Understand the specific acts being attributed to you, and note the deadline.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Check that you have been given enough time.</strong> If dismissal is possible, you should generally have at least five calendar days from receipt. If you are given less, ask in writing for an extension.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Answer in writing.</strong> Respond to each accusation clearly, explain the circumstances, and submit your reply within the period whenever possible.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Stick to the facts.</strong> Avoid emotional or unsupported statements, and point to documents, witnesses, or other evidence that back you up.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Ask for the evidence against you.</strong> If the employer relies on reports, records, CCTV footage, or other materials you need to answer the charge, request reasonable access to them.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Ask for a hearing or conference</strong> if you want to explain in person or there are important factual disputes. Make the request in writing.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Keep copies of everything.</strong> Save the NTE, your explanation, your supporting documents, and proof that you submitted your reply.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Consider getting legal advice,</strong> especially when the accusation involves serious misconduct, dishonesty, fraud, or possible dismissal.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>What if you do not submit an explanation?</strong></p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If you were genuinely given a reasonable opportunity to respond, the employer may go ahead and evaluate the case with the evidence it has. But your silence should not automatically be treated as an admission of guilt. The employer must still decide whether the evidence really proves the violation and whether the penalty is justified.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Likewise, refusing to receive an NTE does not necessarily stop the process. If the employer can show proper service, or document its reasonable attempts to serve the notice, it may proceed under the applicable rules. A refusal, however, does not prove that you did what you are accused of.</p>
<h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Happens If the Employer Skips the NTE?</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This is where the difference between having a valid <em>reason</em> and following a fair <em>process</em> really matters. The consequence depends on whether the employer had a valid legal ground for the dismissal in the first place.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><strong>1. A valid just cause, but no procedural due process.</strong></p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The dismissal generally stands if the employer proves a lawful just cause. But the employer may be ordered to pay <strong>nominal damages</strong> for violating the employee’s statutory right to due process. In <em>Agabon v. NLRC</em> (G.R. No. 158693, November 17, 2004), the Supreme Court set the benchmark at <strong>₱30,000</strong>. This award recognizes the violation of the employee’s right; it is not payment for lost wages, and the amount remains subject to the court’s assessment of the circumstances.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><strong>2. A valid authorized cause, but no proper notice.</strong></p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A similar principle applies to dismissals for authorized causes like redundancy, retrenchment, or closure. In <em>Jaka Food Processing Corp. v. Pacot</em> (G.R. No. 151378, March 28, 2005), the Supreme Court upheld a dismissal based on an established authorized cause but awarded <strong>₱50,000</strong> in nominal damages for failure to comply with the notice requirement.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Under Article 298 of the Labor Code (formerly Article 283), an employer implementing redundancy, retrenchment, or closure must generally give written notice to the affected employee and the proper DOLE office at least one month before the intended termination. It must also prove the authorized cause itself and pay separation pay when the law requires it.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><strong>3. No valid ground at all.</strong></p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This is a different situation. If the employer cannot prove a valid just or authorized cause, the dismissal is illegal, even if it issued an NTE, held a hearing, and prepared a detailed termination letter.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An illegally dismissed employee is generally entitled to reinstatement without loss of seniority rights and to full backwages, including allowances and other benefits, from the time compensation was withheld until actual reinstatement. If reinstatement is no longer viable, separation pay in lieu of reinstatement may be awarded. Damages and attorney’s fees may also be granted when their requirements are met. In some exceptional cases, such as <em>Verizon</em> above, the Court has withheld backwages where dismissal was too harsh but the employer acted in good faith.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><strong>4. A disciplinary suspension imposed without an NTE.</strong></p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The same principle applies to suspensions. In <em>Bohol Wisdom School v. Mabao</em>, the Supreme Court declared the teacher’s suspension illegal on more than one ground, including the employer’s failure to issue a Notice to Explain before imposing it. The school was ordered to pay backwages for the period of the illegal suspension, together with other monetary benefits.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">So the takeaway is simple: following the process cannot cure the absence of a valid ground for dismissal, and having a valid ground does not excuse the employer from following the process.</p>
<h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Quick Reminders for Employers</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Before proceeding with a disciplinary dismissal, keep these basics in mind:</p>
<ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Establish a reasonable factual basis before issuing the NTE.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Clearly state the specific acts or omissions and the applicable legal ground.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">When dismissal is contemplated, give the employee at least five calendar days from receipt to prepare an explanation.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Provide a meaningful chance to respond, present evidence, and request a hearing when appropriate.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Consider the employee’s explanation objectively, and ask whether dismissal is truly justified and proportionate.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Issue a separate written decision explaining your reasons.</li>
<li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Keep records of the investigation, the notices, the proof of service, and the final decision.</li>
</ul>
<h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Final Thoughts</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A Notice to Explain is more than an HR requirement to check off before dismissing someone. It is a core part of the employee’s right to due process.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">For employers, it is a chance to examine the facts, weigh the evidence, and decide whether the alleged violation really warrants disciplinary action. For employees, it is a chance to be heard before a decision about their livelihood is made.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">And here is something employers should never forget: an employee’s explanation may reveal facts management did not know. It may disprove the accusation, offer a reasonable justification, or show that a lighter penalty is more fitting.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>An NTE does not make a dismissal lawful. It begins the process through which an employer decides whether a lawful dismissal is justified.</strong></p>
<p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">If you are an employer or HR practitioner, getting the process right from the start can spare you costly and hard-to-resolve disputes later. And if you are an employee facing disciplinary proceedings, understanding your rights will help you respond properly and protect your interests. Either way, getting advice early is always better than fixing problems later.</p>
