        <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Is Your Manpower Agency a Real Contractor, or Just a Pass-Through?</h3>
        <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published: July 26, 2026 | Category: Employer Compliance</div>

        <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
            <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">A lot of Filipino workers report to a supervisor from one company while their payslip comes from another. That arrangement isn't automatically illegal &mdash; legitimate job contracting is a normal, lawful part of doing business in the Philippines. But when the "contractor" is little more than a name on a payslip, with no real capital, equipment, or control over how the work gets done, the arrangement crosses into labor-only contracting &mdash; and the law treats the company that actually benefits from the work as the real employer, with all the liability that comes with it.</p>

            <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Legal Foundation</h4>
            <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Article 106 of the Labor Code authorizes the Secretary of Labor to regulate contracting and subcontracting arrangements and expressly prohibits labor-only contracting. Articles 107 to 109 address the liability of indirect employers and establish that the principal and the contractor are solidarily liable to the workers for unpaid wages and other claims. The detailed implementing rules are found in DOLE Department Order No. 174, Series of 2017 (issued March 16, 2017), which replaced the older DO 18-A and tightened the standards for who qualifies as a legitimate contractor.</p>

            <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Two-Prong Test for Labor-Only Contracting</h4>
            <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Under Department Order No. 174-17, an arrangement is labor-only contracting &mdash; and therefore prohibited &mdash; when either of the following is present:</p>
            <ol style="padding-left: 24px; margin-bottom: 20px;">
                <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">The contractor does not have substantial capital or investment in the form of tools, equipment, machinery, work premises, and other materials, <strong>and</strong> the workers it supplies perform activities directly related to the principal's main business; or</li>
                <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">The contractor does not exercise the right to control the performance of the workers' actual work &mdash; that is, the "means and methods" by which the job gets done, not just the end result.</li>
            </ol>
            <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court applied both prongs of this test in <em>San Miguel Corporation v. Semillano</em> (G.R. No. 164257, July 5, 2010), where it found labor-only contracting because the workers performed tasks directly related to San Miguel's main business, the manpower cooperative supplying them had no substantial capital of its own, and San Miguel &mdash; not the cooperative &mdash; actually supervised how the work was done and controlled access to its premises. That control over the "means and methods" of the work, not just the contractor's name on the payroll, is what the Court treated as decisive.</p>

            <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What "Substantial Capital" Actually Means</h4>
            <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">DOLE Department Order No. 174-17 sets a concrete financial threshold rather than leaving "substantial" to guesswork. For corporations, partnerships, and cooperatives, substantial capital generally means paid-up capital stock or capital contribution of at least &#8369;5,000,000. Sole proprietorships are held to a comparable net worth requirement. A contractor that can't meet this threshold, and whose workers are doing work directly tied to the principal's core business, fits squarely within the definition of labor-only contracting.</p>

            <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Registration Helps, But It Isn't a Shield</h4>
            <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Contractors covered by DO 174-17 are required to register with the appropriate DOLE Regional Office, and a valid Certificate of Registration is generally good for two years. Failing to register creates a presumption that the contractor is engaged in labor-only contracting. But registration works the other way too: it is not conclusive proof of legitimacy. The Supreme Court has been explicit on this point &mdash; a DOLE Certificate of Registration creates only a disputable presumption, and the actual working arrangement on the ground can overcome it. In <em>Caballero v. Vikings Commissary</em> (G.R. No. 238859, October 19, 2022), the Court looked past the manpower agency's certificate of registration to the totality of the evidence &mdash; who actually supervised the worker, who controlled her schedule, and who effectively terminated her &mdash; in ruling that the agency was a labor-only contractor and Vikings was her real employer.</p>
            <p style="margin: 0 0 15px; padding: 14px 20px; border-left: 3px solid #9C7A3C; background: #f8f9fb; font-style: italic; color: #2d3748;">Determining whether a manpower agency is engaged in legitimate job contracting or labor-only contracting requires looking at all the facts and circumstances. A DOLE Certificate of Registration may help prevent assumptions of labor-only contracting, but it does not serve as conclusive proof of legality.</p>

            <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Legitimate Job Contracting Looks Like</h4>
            <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A contracting arrangement is more likely to hold up as legitimate where the contractor:</p>
            <ul style="padding-left: 24px; margin-bottom: 20px;">
                <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Runs an independent business genuinely distinct from the principal, rather than existing solely to supply that one client with workers.</li>
                <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Has substantial capital or investment of its own in tools, equipment, or work premises.</li>
                <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Actually controls how its employees perform their work &mdash; assigning tasks, setting methods, and supervising output &mdash; rather than simply following the principal's day-to-day direction.</li>
                <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Is properly registered with DOLE and maintains that registration through renewal and reporting.</li>
            </ul>
            <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court illustrated the contrast clearly in <em>Alaska Milk Corporation v. Paez</em> (G.R. Nos. 237277 &amp; 237317, November 27, 2019), a consolidated case involving two agencies supplying workers to the same milk-processing plant. Asiapro Multipurpose Cooperative was found to be a genuine independent contractor &mdash; it had substantial capital, multiple clients, and controlled how its members performed their work. 5S Manpower Services, supplying workers to the same plant, could not show it had any comparable capital or investment of its own, and was found to be a labor-only contractor whose workers were, by operation of law, Alaska's own regular employees.</p>

            <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Happens When Labor-Only Contracting Is Found</h4>
            <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The consequences fall mainly on the principal, not just the labor-only contractor:</p>
            <ul style="padding-left: 24px; margin-bottom: 20px;">
                <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The principal becomes the direct employer</strong> of the supplied workers by operation of law, regardless of what the paperwork says.</li>
                <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Solidary liability</strong> attaches for unpaid wages, statutory benefits, and other monetary claims &mdash; the principal cannot point to the contractor as the only party responsible.</li>
                <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Regularization</strong> may be ordered where the workers have been performing core, ongoing business functions.</li>
                <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Closure orders and administrative fines</strong> can be issued against the labor-only contractor itself.</li>
            </ul>

            <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Practical Guidance</h4>
            <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>For companies engaging contractors:</strong> Due diligence matters before signing, not after a labor complaint is filed. Verify the contractor's DOLE registration, ask for evidence of actual capital and equipment, and confirm that the contractor &mdash; not your own supervisors &mdash; is the one directing how the supplied workers do their jobs day to day. A registration certificate alone will not protect you if the arrangement doesn't reflect genuine independence on the ground.</p>
            <p style="margin-bottom: 0;"><strong>For workers placed by an agency:</strong> Who actually supervises your work, sets your schedule, and disciplines you matters more than whose name is on your payslip. If the company you report to every day controls how you do your job, and the agency has little more than a name and a payroll function, you may have grounds to be recognized as a regular employee of the principal company itself.</p>
