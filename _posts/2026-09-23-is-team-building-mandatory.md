    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Is Team Building Mandatory? What Philippine Labor Law Actually Says</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on September 23, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">"Team building" is one of those workplace expressions that everyone uses but no law actually defines. There is no provision in the Labor Code that says "team building is mandatory" or "team building is voluntary." So which is it? The honest answer is: it depends on how the employer structures the activity — whether attendance is genuinely required, whether the activity is work-related, when it takes place, and what the applicable employment policies and labor standards require.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">There may be no single statute specifically regulating team-building events, but that does not mean they exist outside labor law. Depending on the circumstances, rules on management prerogative, hours worked, overtime, rest days, holidays, occupational safety and health, wage deductions, and Employees' Compensation may all become relevant.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Can your employer really make you go?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Employers do have "management prerogative" — the right to run their business and establish reasonable rules for how work is carried out. But that prerogative is not unlimited. It must be exercised reasonably, lawfully, in good faith, and consistently with rights protected by law, applicable agreements, and established company policies.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If a team building is genuinely connected to work — for example, an activity designed to improve communication, coordination, or cooperation among employees — and it is held during regular working hours, the employer may generally require attendance as it may require attendance at other legitimate work-related activities. Calling something "team building" does not by itself make the activity voluntary.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">But calling something "team building" also does not give an employer unlimited authority to compel employees to surrender their personal time without regard to labor standards. Timing is therefore one of the most important factors in determining the legal consequences.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What if it falls on a rest day or a holiday?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A scheduled rest day and a regular holiday are not treated the same way under the Labor Code.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">For a scheduled rest day, Article 92 identifies specific circumstances in which an employer may require an employee to work — including certain emergencies, urgent work necessary to prevent serious loss, abnormal pressure of work, continuous operations, and analogous situations. A routine team-building activity would not ordinarily fall within those statutory exceptions simply because management wants employees to attend.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A regular holiday is different. Article 94 expressly allows an employer to require an employee to work on a holiday, subject to the applicable holiday-pay rules. Thus, a mandatory team-building activity on a regular holiday is not automatically unlawful merely because it occurs on a holiday. The important questions are whether the activity constitutes work, whether the employee is covered by the applicable labor standards, and whether the required holiday compensation is properly paid.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">For an ordinary team-building activity scheduled on a rest day, the safer approach is generally to make attendance voluntary unless a lawful exception permits the employer to require work. If employees voluntarily attend, the employer should still determine whether the time qualifies as compensable work under the applicable rules.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What if you just do not show up?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If attendance was genuinely mandatory, the activity was held on a regular working day, and an employee skips it without a valid reason, the employer is not necessarily powerless. But it also cannot simply discipline the employee on the spot.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The employer must be able to establish that attendance was actually required, that the instruction was lawful and reasonable, that the employee knew about it, and that the employee deliberately failed to comply without a valid justification. If discipline is imposed, the applicable substantive and procedural due-process requirements must be observed.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A company handbook, employment contract, established policy, or collective bargaining agreement may also matter. These documents can help determine whether employees were given reasonable notice of the attendance requirement and what disciplinary consequences, if any, may be imposed.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Genuine health, disability, religious, safety, or other legally protected concerns should be raised promptly and considered on their own merits. Not every personal reason automatically creates a legal right to skip a required activity, but legitimate protected concerns deserve real consideration and, where the law requires it, an appropriate accommodation or alternative.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Do you get paid for the time you spend there?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If the activity is mandatory and takes place during what would otherwise be the employee's normal working hours, that time generally counts as hours worked. The fact that employees are participating in games, discussions, or other team activities rather than performing their ordinary tasks does not by itself make the time non-compensable.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If the required activity extends beyond the employee's normal hours, the applicable overtime rules may apply to covered employees. If it occurs on a scheduled rest day or holiday, the applicable premium or holiday-pay rules must likewise be considered.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">There is an important qualification: not everyone is covered by the Labor Code's ordinary hours-of-work and overtime provisions. Managerial employees and certain field personnel, among others, are subject to different rules. Overtime or premium entitlement is therefore not automatically the same for every position in the company.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">There is also a specific rule concerning lectures, meetings, training, and similar activities. Attendance outside regular working hours may be excluded from working time when attendance is genuinely voluntary and the other requirements of the rule are satisfied. That distinction matters: an activity that employees are required to attend cannot simply be characterized as "voluntary training" or "team building" to avoid the rules on working time.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Travel time to and from the venue is more complicated. Whether it is compensable depends on the particular circumstances, including where employees are required to report, whether the travel is an integral part of the required activity, and whether the employee is required to remain under the employer's direction during the travel. There is no one-size-fits-all rule.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Who is supposed to pay for it?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">There is no specific Labor Code provision that simply says, "If the employer requires team building, the employer must pay for the venue, transportation, and meals." What the law does clearly regulate are deductions from wages. An employer cannot simply pass the cost of an employer-organized activity to employees through unauthorized payroll deductions. Wage deductions are subject to the limitations imposed by law and applicable regulations.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">As a matter of sound HR practice rather than a blanket statutory requirement, an employer that requires employees to attend an employer-organized team-building activity should ordinarily consider shouldering reasonable transportation, meals, accommodation, and activity costs. Making mandatory participation financially painless is sensible management practice even when the law does not expressly assign every event expense to the employer.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What about safety?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A team building does not stop being subject to occupational safety and health considerations simply because it takes place outside the office or is described as recreational. Depending on the circumstances, a company-organized off-site activity may fall within the scope of applicable OSH requirements where employees are required to go there because of their work and the statutory conditions are present.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This matters particularly for activities such as ziplining, wall climbing, rappelling, obstacle courses, hiking, and water activities. If employees are required to participate because of their work, the employer should assess the hazards, provide appropriate safety instructions and precautions, and ensure that the activity is conducted in a reasonably safe environment. Hiring a third-party activity provider does not automatically eliminate the employer's own responsibilities.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What if someone actually gets hurt?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An injury during an employer-organized, employer-directed activity does not automatically become compensable simply because the company arranged it. But it is not automatically excluded either. Whether an injury qualifies for Employees' Compensation benefits depends on the specific facts and on whether the injury is considered work-connected and arose out of and in the course of employment under the applicable Employees' Compensation rules.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Employees' Compensation Commission has in fact granted benefits in a case involving an employee who was injured on his way home after a company team-building activity. In that case, the ECC found the injury compensable based on the particular circumstances, including the employee's route home immediately after the activity and the applicable "going to and coming from work" rule. The lesson is not that every injury connected to team building is automatically compensable. It is that the label "team building" does not automatically disqualify a claim either. Each case turns on its own facts.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Separate from Employees' Compensation, civil liability may arise if someone's negligence causes injury. For example, if a company driver negligently causes an accident while transporting employees to the venue, the employer may potentially face civil liability under the applicable rules, subject to the facts and any defenses available to it, including the employer's due diligence in the selection and supervision of employees.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Criminal liability is personal. Depending on the facts, a person whose own negligent act or omission causes injury or death may incur criminal liability under the applicable provisions of the Revised Penal Code. Simply holding a management position does not, by itself, establish criminal responsibility.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">And what about waivers? Companies sometimes ask employees to sign an "assumption of risk" or liability waiver before activities such as ziplining or rappelling. These documents can be relevant in a dispute, but they are not an absolute shield against liability. They do not automatically erase statutory rights or excuse unlawful conduct or negligence. Their effect depends on their language, the circumstances in which they were signed, and the applicable law.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The bottom line</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Team building is neither automatically mandatory nor automatically voluntary. The legal answer depends on how the activity is structured.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A useful checklist is simple: Is attendance genuinely required or optional in practice? Is the activity actually work-related, and is the directive reasonable and lawful? When is it scheduled — during regular working hours, on a rest day, or on a holiday? Are the employees covered by the applicable hours-of-work and premium-pay rules? Who is expected to cover the cost? And if something goes wrong, what occupational safety, Employees' Compensation, civil, or criminal rules may apply?</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Calling something "team building" does not switch off these legal questions. Once attendance becomes mandatory, the ordinary rules that apply to the particular employment situation — including rules on working time, rest days, holidays, occupational safety, wage deductions, and compensation for work-connected injuries — remain relevant. The label on the activity does not change the law.</p>
