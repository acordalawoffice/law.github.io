    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Can Illness Cost You Your Job? The Truth About Termination Due to Disease</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on November 14, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">It starts with a doctor's appointment. Then comes a diagnosis, a few days of rest, maybe a long recovery. And somewhere in the middle, a quiet worry takes hold: <em>Will I still have a job when I get better?</em> It is a fair question, and the law has a clear answer. But the answer is not a simple yes or no.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">In this article, we walk through when an employer may lawfully end the employment of a sick employee, what the employer must do first, and what happens when those steps are skipped. Whether you are an employee worried about your future or an employer unsure about what to do, this guide is for you.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Short Answer: Yes, but Only Under Strict Conditions</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Labor Code recognizes disease as one of the lawful reasons for ending employment (Article 299, formerly Article 284). The law calls this an "authorized cause." It simply means that the employee did nothing wrong. Nobody is being punished. The law merely recognizes that, in some situations, continued work is no longer possible or safe.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Because the employee is not at fault, the burden rests on the employer. It is the employer who bears the burden of proving, through substantial evidence, that the legal requirements for termination have been satisfied. A good intention is not a substitute for proof.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">When Is Termination Allowed?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Not every illness qualifies. The law allows termination only when the employee suffers from a disease and, as a result, continued employment is either:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">prohibited by law, or</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">harmful to the employee's own health or to the health of co-workers.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A common cold, a condition that is well managed with medicine, or a few absences covered by sick leave will not, standing alone, establish a valid ground for dismissal. Being ill, by itself, is not a ground for dismissal. Even a worker in a sensitive job, such as food handling, may be let go only if the specific illness and the applicable health regulations truly make continued work unsafe or unlawful.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What the Employer Must Do First</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">For a termination due to disease to be legally defensible, the employer must establish the substantive requirements, observe fair procedure, and pay the separation benefits required by law.</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A medical certificate from a competent public health authority.</strong> The employer cannot decide for itself how serious an employee's illness is. Under Section 5.4(e), Rule I-A of DOLE Department Order No. 147-15, three things must be present: the employee suffers from a disease; continued work is prohibited by law or harmful to the health of the employee or co-workers; and a certification from a competent public health authority, such as a government doctor or health officer, confirms that the disease cannot be cured within six months even with proper treatment. The diagnosis or fitness-to-work opinion of the company doctor, or a certificate from a private clinic, may not be enough on its own. The Supreme Court has not fully defined who counts as a competent public health authority, so employers should be careful and seek advice.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Two written notices.</strong> The Supreme Court has ruled that even a termination due to disease requires fair procedure. The employer must first give the employee a written notice explaining the reason for the proposed termination. The employee must then be given a real chance to respond and be heard. Only after that may the employer issue a second written notice informing the employee of the decision.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Separation pay.</strong> The employee must receive at least one month's pay, or one-half month's pay for every year of service, whichever is higher. A fraction of at least six months counts as one full year. For example, an employee with 10 years of service would ordinarily receive five months' pay, since that is greater than one month's pay.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The medical certificate is the requirement most often overlooked. In <em>Deoferio v. Intel Technology Philippines, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 202996, June 18, 2014), the Supreme Court treated it as the main proof of the illness, of its incurability within six months, and of its harmful effect on the employee and co-workers, and described it as a substantive requirement, not a mere formality. Without it, the Court said, there is no valid ground for dismissal. The Court found that the report of a consultant psychiatrist at the Philippine General Hospital substantially proved the illness, and it confirmed that the two-notice procedure applies to terminations due to disease. The Court had earlier emphasized the same points in <em>Sy v. Court of Appeals</em> (G.R. No. 142293, February 27, 2003) and <em>Manly Express, Inc. v. Payong, Jr.</em> (G.R. No. 167462, October 25, 2005), and it repeated them in <em>SRL International Manpower Agency v. Yarza</em> (G.R. No. 207828, February 14, 2022), where the dismissal was struck down for lack of the required certification, notice, and hearing, and where a certificate from an accredited private clinic was not accepted.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">There is also an important regulatory consideration. Section 5.3, Rule I-A of DOLE Department Order No. 147-15 provides that, for authorized causes, due process is deemed complied with upon a written notice to the employee and to the appropriate DOLE Regional Office at least 30 days before termination. Disease is one of the authorized causes. The same Section 5 also states that in all cases of termination, the standards of due process under the Labor Code and settled jurisprudence must be observed. Before this Order, the rules required the 30-day notice only for causes such as closure and retrenchment, and the Court filled the gap for disease with the two-notice rule. This provision has raised questions about how it fits with the Supreme Court's two-notice rulings in <em>Deoferio</em> and <em>SRL International</em>. Although the Court repeated the two-notice requirement in 2022, the termination in <em>SRL International</em> took place in 2011, before the Department Order was issued in 2015. The Supreme Court has not expressly resolved how Section 5.3 relates to its rulings on fair procedure in disease-related dismissals. As a practical safeguard, employers should observe both: the 30-day notice required by the Department Order and the two-notice procedure with a real chance to be heard required by the Supreme Court.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What If the Illness Can Be Cured Within Six Months?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Then the employer may not terminate the employee on this ground. Under Section 8, Rule I, Book VI of the Omnibus Rules Implementing the Labor Code, the employee should instead be asked to take a leave of absence to undergo treatment. Once the employee recovers, the employer must take the employee back to the former position.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A word of caution: the six-month figure is not a limit on sick leave, and the mere passing of six months does not allow an employer to dismiss an employee. What matters is the medical nature and stage of the disease, whether it can be cured even with proper treatment, and the harm or legal prohibition that continued work would bring.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Happens If the Employer Gets It Wrong?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The consequences depend on what exactly went wrong, and the difference matters.</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>No valid ground.</strong> If the employer terminates without the required medical certificate, the law treats the ground as not proven, and the dismissal is illegal. The employee may be entitled to reinstatement and backwages, which are the salaries the employee would have earned from the day of dismissal. Other awards, such as damages and attorney's fees, depend on the circumstances of the case.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Valid ground, defective procedure.</strong> If the employer establishes a valid ground but fails to observe the applicable procedural requirements, including the required notices and a meaningful opportunity for the employee to be heard, the dismissal may still stand. However, the employer may be required to pay nominal damages for violating the employee's right to procedural due process. In <em>Deoferio</em>, the Supreme Court awarded ₱30,000 for this violation, after weighing factors such as the employer's medical and financial help, the leave it allowed, and its genuine attempt to give notice.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Unpaid separation pay.</strong> If the dismissal is otherwise valid but the separation pay is not paid, the employee remains entitled to collect it.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Employers should also be careful about discrimination. Several special laws protect workers: Republic Act No. 7277 (as amended, Section 32) prohibits discrimination in employment against qualified persons with disabilities, and requires an employer to first try reasonable accommodations before dismissing an employee because of a disability, Republic Act No. 11166 (Section 49) prohibits employment discrimination based on actual, perceived, or suspected HIV status, and Republic Act No. 11036 (Section 25) requires employers to adopt workplace policies that address the stigma and discrimination faced by persons with mental health conditions. Section 6 of DOLE Department Order No. 147-15 itself adds that an employee may not be terminated based on actual, perceived, or suspected HIV or Hepatitis B status, and that an employee who has or had tuberculosis may not be discriminated against and must be restored to work once the illness is controlled. These rules provide different forms of protection, but they share an important principle: an employer cannot lawfully dismiss an employee merely because of a medical diagnosis or health condition. The legal requirements and protections must always be considered.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Practical Tips</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>For employers:</strong></p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Document everything, from medical records to every notice sent.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Secure the required certification from a competent public health authority before proceeding with the termination process.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Give the first notice, listen to the employee's side, and only then issue the decision in writing.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Serve the 30-day written notice on the employee and the appropriate DOLE Regional Office, as required by the Department Order.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Consider leave, a lighter assignment, or other reasonable adjustments. These may not always be strictly required for this ground, but they are the humane choice and, for employees with disabilities, may be required by law.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Seek legal advice before issuing any notice.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>For employees:</strong></p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Keep copies of your medical records, certificates, and sick leave requests.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Respond to any notice from your employer in writing and on time.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Check whether a proper medical certificate was actually obtained and whether you were given a real chance to be heard.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Keep your employer informed about your treatment and recovery. It is simply good practice and helps avoid misunderstandings.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Seek advice early, because the time to question a dismissal is limited.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Closing Thoughts</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Illness is part of life, and the law treats it that way. It leaves room for an employer to end the employment of a seriously ill worker, but it demands proof, fair procedure, and separation pay before doing so. A health challenge should be met with compassion and due process, not a sudden pink slip.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">If you are an employee facing a dismissal because of illness, or an employer deciding how to handle a sick member of your team, a conversation with a labor lawyer before acting can save a great deal of trouble for everyone involved.</p>
