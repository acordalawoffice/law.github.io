    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">One Notice or Two? The Legal Trap Employers Fall Into When Firing Probationary Employees</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on August 10, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Here's a scenario that trips up even seasoned HR teams: a probationary employee isn't just underperforming — he's also caused a string of costly mistakes. Is that a regularization issue? A misconduct issue? Turns out, the answer changes everything about how you're supposed to let him go.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Get it wrong, and you're not just facing an awkward exit conversation. You could be looking at an illegal dismissal complaint, or at the very least, a nominal damages award for skipping the wrong kind of due process. So before you draft that termination letter, it helps to slow down and understand what probationary employment actually is, what the law expects from you as the employer, and — most importantly — which of two very different roads you're actually walking down.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Probationary Employment Really Is</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Probationary employment is a trial period, capped at six months under the Labor Code. It exists so both sides can find out if the fit is right — the employer gets to observe the employee's actual performance, and the employee gets a shot at proving they belong in the role permanently. It's the exception to the general rule of regular employment, not a lesser version of it. That six-month cap is a ceiling, not a promise. It tells the employer how long they have to make a decision — it doesn't mean the employee is untouchable until day 180.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Before You Even Sign: The One Thing That Determines Everything</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Here's the part employers most often get wrong, and it happens before the employee even starts working: the standards for regularization must be clearly communicated at the time of hiring. Ideally, this means spelling them out in the employment contract or during orientation — what does "meeting expectations" actually look like for this role, in concrete terms?</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Skip this step, and you lose your strongest ground for termination later. If the standards were never disclosed, the law treats the employee as regular from day one — full security of tenure, no probationary exit available at all. This single requirement is the foundation everything else in this article rests on.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Fork in the Road: Two Different Grounds</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Once you're at the point of deciding whether to let a probationary employee go, you're usually choosing between two distinct legal grounds — and they are not interchangeable.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Ground 1: Failure to Qualify for Regularization.</strong> This is a performance and fitness assessment, not a disciplinary charge. It simply asks: did this employee meet the standards that were made known at the start? If not, the employer can end the engagement any time within the six-month period — you don't have to wait until the last day. The process is light: one written notice explaining the non-qualification, served before the termination takes effect. No formal hearing, no need for the employee's written explanation.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;"><strong>Ground 2: Just Cause under Article 297.</strong> This covers any of the just causes recognized under the Labor Code — serious misconduct, willful disobedience, fraud or breach of trust, commission of a crime against the employer, gross and habitual neglect of duties, and other analogous causes. Gross and habitual neglect is simply one common illustration; the same heavier process applies no matter which of these grounds is actually invoked. This is a disciplinary ground, available to any employee, probationary or not, and it comes with a much heavier process: a Notice to Explain describing the specific acts or omissions, at least five days for the employee to respond in writing, a hearing or conference if the facts are contested, and finally a written notice of the decision.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Side by Side: Why the Difference Matters</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Failure to qualify: one written notice, no hearing required, timeline is flexible as long as it lands before termination and before the six-month mark. Documentation needed is the disclosed standards plus a fair performance assessment against them.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Just cause (any Article 297 ground, such as gross and habitual neglect, serious misconduct, or breach of trust): full twin-notice process, minimum five days for the employee's explanation, a hearing if warranted, and documentation needs to cover each specific act or incident in detail — dates, what happened, and how it connects to the specific just cause being invoked. Skip any step here and the dismissal can be found procedurally defective, even if the underlying reason was valid.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Trap: You Can't Switch Grounds Later</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This is where employers get caught. Say you chose the simpler route — failure to qualify — and issued that one notice. The employee later challenges the dismissal. Can you then argue, "well, those incidents actually amounted to a just cause too, so it was justified either way"?</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">No. Once you've picked a ground and processed the termination under it, that's the ground your case will be judged on. Labor tribunals don't allow employers to introduce a stronger legal theory after the fact, especially one that requires due process steps that were never actually taken. If you never issued a Notice to Explain citing the specific just cause, never gave five days to respond, never held a hearing — you can't retroactively claim that ground just because the facts might have supported it, whether the underlying just cause was gross and habitual neglect, serious misconduct, or any other cause under Article 297. The employee was never given the chance to defend against that specific charge, and that's precisely what due process is meant to protect.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The negligence incidents can still be used — but only as supporting evidence for the ground you actually invoked, not as an independent backup defense you pull out later.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Practical Tips That Strengthen Your Position</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">None of the following is strictly required by law, but each one makes a real difference if the termination is ever questioned before the NLRC or a labor arbiter. Think of these as the difference between a decision that merely survives scrutiny and one that's genuinely well-defended.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Put the standards in writing, and have the employee acknowledge them.</strong> A signed acknowledgment — even a simple line in the employment contract or orientation form — removes any doubt later about whether the standards were actually communicated. Verbal briefings are easy to dispute; paper trails are not.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Do more than one evaluation, even if the law doesn't require it.</strong> A single assessment at the tail end of the decision invites the argument that it was arranged to justify a decision already made. Interim check-ins — say, at the 30-day and 60-day marks — build a consistent, dated record showing the standards were applied over time, not invoked only when convenient.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Give informal feedback along the way.</strong> Coaching isn't legally mandated for failure-to-qualify cases, but employees who received zero feedback before the final notice are far more likely to feel blindsided — and far more likely to file a complaint. A documented conversation, even brief, shows good faith.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Keep the notice title and language non-disciplinary.</strong> If you're proceeding on failure to qualify, avoid language that sounds like an accusation of wrongdoing. Stick to performance terms — "did not meet the standards for X, Y, Z" — rather than characterizations that blur the line into a disciplinary charge you haven't actually processed.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Have the employee sign an acknowledgment of receipt for the notice itself.</strong> If they refuse to sign, have a witness note the date and manner of service. This closes off any later claim that notice was never properly given.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Apply your standards consistently across employees.</strong> If two probationary employees had similar shortfalls but only one was let go, that inconsistency can undercut the claim that the decision was a fair, good-faith assessment rather than a selective or arbitrary one.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Don't let the termination date creep past the six-month mark.</strong> Build in a buffer. If the plan is to conclude by day 180, aim to finalize the decision and serve notice well before that — a last-minute scramble risks the employee being deemed regularized simply because the clock ran out before the paperwork did.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Get a second, independent review for genuinely borderline cases.</strong> If the evidence for either ground feels thin, or if the case involves both performance and misconduct issues that could tip either way, a quick legal review before the notice goes out is far cheaper than defending a contested dismissal later.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;"><strong>Don't forget final pay and clearance.</strong> Even a valid, well-documented termination can generate a separate money claim if final pay, pro-rated 13th month, and the Certificate of Employment aren't released within the timelines set by DOLE guidance. A clean termination on the merits can still turn into a dispute over money if this last administrative step is dropped.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A Simple Checklist Before You Decide</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">1. Were the regularization standards clearly disclosed to this employee at hiring, ideally with a signed acknowledgment? If no, you likely don't have a failure-to-qualify case at all.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">2. Is the problem an overall performance shortfall, or specific incidents serious enough to stand alone as negligence? This tells you which ground actually fits the facts.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">3. Do you have more than a single, last-minute evaluation on record? Interim documentation is your best defense against a claim of bad faith.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">4. Are you prepared to follow the full process the chosen ground requires — and only that ground — from notice to effective date, with the paperwork and final pay to match? Decide before you act, not while you're already mid-termination.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Ending a probationary employee's engagement early isn't illegal on its own — the law allows it, and it doesn't require retraining, coaching, or a lengthy notice period beforehand. What it does require is choosing the right ground from the start and following through on the process that ground demands. Get that part right, and a probationary termination is one of the most straightforward exits under Philippine labor law. Get it backwards, and even a valid reason for letting someone go can turn into a costly, avoidable dispute.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Quick Question: Can an Employer Invoke Both Grounds at the Same Time?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Yes. The two grounds rest on different legal bases even when they come from the same set of facts. Failure to qualify looks at overall performance against the disclosed standards; just cause looks at specific documented acts that independently rise to one of the causes under Article 297. The same incidents can support both, and an employer can cite both in a single notice.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The catch: once a just cause is invoked at all, the full twin-notice process governs the entire termination — there's no mixing the lighter failure-to-qualify notice with a shortcut on the disciplinary side. Some employers prefer to lean on just cause alone when the documentation is solid, since it doesn't depend on questions about whether standards were properly disclosed. Others prefer failure to qualify alone, treating the incidents as supporting evidence rather than a separate ground, to keep the process simpler. Either way, the choice has to be made upfront — and followed through consistently, since it can't be changed after the fact.</p>
