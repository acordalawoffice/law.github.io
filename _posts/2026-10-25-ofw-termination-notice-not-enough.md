    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Laid Off Abroad? Why a One-Month Notice Alone Cannot End Your OFW Contract</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on October 25, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Picture this. You have been working abroad for almost a year, doing your job well and sending money home. Then one day, your employer hands you a letter: your contract will end in 30 days. The reason? Business has been slow and there are not enough projects. You look at your contract, and yes, it does say that either side may end it by giving one month's written notice.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">So, is that the end of the story? The Supreme Court says no. In <em>I-People Manpower Resources, Inc. v. Court of Appeals and Monton</em> (G.R. No. 246410, January 25, 2023), the Court made it clear that a clause allowing termination on one month's notice does not, by itself, make an early termination lawful.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Notice and a valid reason are two different things. The employer must still show a legally valid ground for ending the contract and follow the required steps. Let us walk through what happened and what it means for you.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Happened</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Jomer Monton was hired as an electrical engineer by a company in Qatar through a Philippine manpower agency. His contract ran for two years, from November 2013 to November 2015, with a monthly basic salary of QAR 6,000 plus an allowance of QAR 3,000. The contract allowed the employer to end it by giving one month's written notice.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">After about eleven months of work, Jomer received a letter dated October 6, 2014, telling him that his contract would be terminated within 30 days because of low activity in the company and a lack of projects. He was sent home on November 10, 2014, with nearly a year left on his contract. He then filed a complaint for illegal dismissal.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The Labor Arbiter and the National Labor Relations Commission sided with the employer, reasoning that the contract allowed termination with a month's notice, so no particular reason was needed. The Court of Appeals disagreed, and the Supreme Court agreed with the Court of Appeals.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Your Rights Can Travel With You</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court reminded everyone that the rights given to Filipino workers by the Constitution and the Labor Code do not disappear simply because the work is done abroad. Your right to keep your job unless there is a valid reason to let you go does not disappear at the airport.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In Jomer's case, the Court applied Philippine labor law because his employment contract was perfected in the Philippines. Which country's law applies can depend on the circumstances of each case, including where the contract was made, so it is worth having your own contract and situation reviewed.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The Court also explained that a contract cannot cancel out the law. Employers and employees do not stand on equal footing, so labor contracts are treated differently from ordinary contracts. The protections of the law are considered part of every employment contract, even if they are not written in it. In other words, that "one month's notice" clause has to be read together with the law, and it cannot be used to skip the legal requirements for ending someone's job.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">"Slow Business" Has to Be Proven</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The employer called it "retrenchment," which simply means cutting down on employees to save the business. This is a legitimate reason to let workers go, and slow business or a lack of projects can be a real concern. But retrenchment is treated as a last resort, and the employer must establish three things:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A genuine business need.</strong> The retrenchment must be reasonably necessary and likely to prevent business losses. If losses have already been incurred, they must be substantial, serious, actual, and real.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Good faith.</strong> It must be done to address a real business need, and not as a way to get around the worker's right to keep his job.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Fair selection.</strong> A fair and reasonable method must be used to decide who goes and who stays.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">For retrenchment, the employer must also give written notice to both the worker and the Department of Labor and Employment regional office, at least one month before the termination, stating the reason. The Court applied this rule to Jomer's case because Philippine law governed his contract.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The employer only said that business was slow and projects were few. It did not back this up with proof, and the Court said that was not enough. Once a worker shows that he was dismissed, it is the employer who must prove that there was a valid reason and that the proper steps were followed.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A Polite "Thank You" Is Not a Waiver</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Before leaving, Jomer sent a courteous email to the company's managing director, thanking him for the guidance and the learning experience. The employer argued that this showed Jomer had accepted his termination.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The Court did not agree. It called the email a simple act of courtesy and an expression of appreciation. A friendly goodbye, or even an acknowledgment that your services have ended, does not by itself mean you gave up your right to question whether the dismissal was legal. Of course, the legal effect of any document you sign depends on what it says and the circumstances in which it was made, so read carefully before you sign anything.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Jomer Received</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court ordered the foreign employer, the Philippine recruitment agency, and the agency's corporate officer, in his capacity as corporate officer, to pay Jomer the following awards, subject to the applicable interest:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">QAR 72,000, representing his salaries for the unfinished part of his contract, with legal interest;</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">a refund of the placement fees he paid, with interest; and</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">attorney's fees equal to 10% of the total award, with further interest until everything is fully paid.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">These were the awards in Jomer's case. What another OFW can recover will depend on the applicable law, the contract, the evidence, and the circumstances of the dismissal. In fact, the Court did not award moral and exemplary damages in this case because it found no bad faith. It saw the employer as simply trying to use the termination clause in the contract. Winning an illegal dismissal case does not automatically mean every kind of damages will be awarded.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What This Means for You</h4>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Keep your paper trail.</strong> Save your contract, termination letter, payslips, emails, and receipts for any placement fees.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Ask for the reason in writing.</strong> If your employer ends your contract early, ask for the stated ground and keep a copy of the notice. Where Philippine labor law applies, the employer must establish a legally valid ground and comply with the required steps.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Read before you sign.</strong> If you are handed papers around the time of your repatriation, take a moment and ask for advice if you are unsure.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Being polite does not mean giving up your rights.</strong> A gracious goodbye will not, by itself, cost you your claim.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Do not wait too long.</strong> Claims have time limits, so seek guidance as soon as you can.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Employers and recruitment agencies can also learn from this case. The Supreme Court rejected their challenge not only because the termination was unsupported by sufficient evidence, but also because they did not pursue the proper remedy within the required period or fully comply with procedural requirements. A defensible employment decision needs both a valid reason and careful attention to procedure.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Bottom Line</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">A contract is important, but it is not the last word. Even when your contract allows early termination with notice, an employer cannot rely on that clause alone. Where Philippine labor law applies, it still needs a valid reason, proof of that reason, and proper notice. If you have been sent home early and something does not feel right, it is worth finding out where you stand. Every case depends on its own facts, so talking to a lawyer about your specific situation is always a good first step.</p>
