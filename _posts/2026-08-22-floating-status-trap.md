    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">The "Floating Status" Trap: When Layoff Isn't Really a Layoff</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on August 22, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

<div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If you run a security agency, a manpower company, or any business that assigns people to different client sites, you've probably used the term "floating status" without thinking twice about it. A client ends its contract, there's no new post ready yet, so the guard or worker is told to wait for the next assignment. It may feel harmless. It may genuinely be temporary. And in the right circumstances, the law recognizes that it can be.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">But there is an important catch: <strong>floating status is temporary by design.</strong> The law does not give an employer an indefinite period to keep an employee without work or assignment. There is a legal clock — and employers need to know both when it starts and what they are expected to do before it runs out.</p>

    <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">So what exactly is floating status?</h4>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Floating status, sometimes called "temporary off-detail," happens when an employee is temporarily without an assignment but has not been terminated. It commonly arises when a security agency's client ends or does not renew a service contract, or when a manpower or service provider has no immediate client post to which the employee can be deployed.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The important point is this: <strong>floating status is not the same as termination.</strong> During a valid period of temporary suspension, the employment relationship continues. The employee is simply not rendering work for the time being.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">That is why the arrangement has limits. The law allows temporary displacement in appropriate circumstances, but it does not allow an employer to use "floating status" as a permanent holding area for employees it no longer wants to employ.</p>

    <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Why does the law allow it?</h4>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Article 301 of the Labor Code recognizes that a bona fide suspension of the operation of a business or undertaking for a period not exceeding six months does not terminate employment. The Supreme Court has applied this principle by analogy to employees placed on temporary layoff or floating status, particularly in the security-services industry.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In security services, for example, assignments depend heavily on contracts with third-party clients. When a client ends a contract and there is genuinely no available post, temporarily placing the affected guard on off-detail status may therefore be a legitimate exercise of management prerogative.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">But there is an important qualification: <strong>the six-month period is a maximum period, not a six-month entitlement.</strong> An employer cannot simply say, "I have six months, so I can keep the employee floating for six months regardless of the circumstances." The temporary suspension must have a legitimate basis and must be exercised in good faith. The Supreme Court has specifically cautioned that floating status cannot be used indefinitely or as a device to defeat security of tenure.</p>

    <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The six-month rule</h4>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">As a general rule, an employee's floating status should not exceed six months. If an employee remains without work or assignment beyond the permissible period, the employer may face a finding of constructive dismissal.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">But this is where an important legal nuance comes in: <strong>the mere passage of six months does not automatically establish constructive dismissal in every factual situation.</strong> The Supreme Court has explained that the circumstances surrounding the employee's failure to assume another assignment must still be examined.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">For example, an employer may be able to avoid liability where it actually offered the employee a legitimate alternative assignment within the six-month period, but the employee refused it for reasons unrelated to any unlawful demotion or diminution of pay or benefits. On the other hand, an employer cannot simply send a general instruction to "report to the office" and treat that as a genuine reassignment when no specific post or actual work is offered.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In <em>Loque v. Seventh Fleet Security Services, Inc., G.R. No. 230005, January 22, 2020</em>, the Supreme Court found constructive dismissal where the employee remained on floating status for more than six months and the employer's communications did not actually redeploy him to a specific assignment. The Court emphasized that the employer had failed to show a genuine reassignment within the applicable period.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The lesson is simple: <strong>do not treat the six-month rule as a stopwatch that automatically decides the case. Treat it as a hard legal boundary that requires the employer to act before the boundary is crossed.</strong></p>

    <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What must the employer be able to prove?</h4>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If the employee challenges the arrangement, the employer may have to prove that the floating status was genuine. In security-agency cases, the Supreme Court has placed on the employer the burden of showing that there was no available post to which the security guard could be assigned.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">That means saying "there was no available client" may not be enough. The employer should be prepared to show the circumstances behind that claim.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 10px;">Depending on the circumstances, useful records may include:</p>

    <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
        <li style="margin-bottom: 7px;">client contracts that ended or were not renewed;</li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 7px;">records of available and unavailable posts;</li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 7px;">deployment and reassignment records;</li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 7px;">communications with clients concerning staffing requirements;</li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 7px;">specific assignment offers made to the employee;</li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 7px;">the employee's response to those offers;</li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 7px;">notices informing the employee of the temporary status and subsequent developments; and</li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 7px;">other records showing that the employer genuinely attempted to return the employee to productive work.</li>
    </ul>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Good faith is important, but documentation is what allows an employer to demonstrate that good faith when the arrangement is later questioned.</p>

    <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What should employers do while the employee is waiting?</h4>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">First, make sure there is a genuine reason for the temporary displacement. In the security-services context, this may involve a client contract ending or a temporary surplus of guards because available assignments have fallen below the number of employees awaiting deployment.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Second, actually look for a legitimate reassignment. If a suitable post becomes available, consider offering it to the employee rather than simply allowing the floating status to continue.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Third, communicate. Let the employee know what is happening, maintain appropriate records, and avoid creating the impression that the employee has simply been forgotten.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">And fourth, calendar the six-month deadline from the beginning. <strong>The six-month date is not the date to start thinking about what to do.</strong> It is the deadline that should already have triggered a decision about recall, reassignment, or a lawful separation based on a valid authorized cause, where applicable.</p>

    <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What happens when six months is approaching?</h4>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The employer should not simply wait for the six-month period to expire and hope that a new assignment will eventually appear.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If a legitimate assignment is available, the employee should generally be recalled or reassigned, subject of course to the employer's lawful management prerogative and the terms of the employment relationship.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If there is no available work and the business circumstances justify ending the employment relationship, the employer must consider whether a valid authorized cause exists and, if so, comply with the substantive and procedural requirements applicable to that authorized cause. This may include the required notices and separation pay when the law requires it.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The point is that <strong>letting the six-month period simply expire is not a substitute for making a lawful employment decision.</strong></p>

    <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A practical checklist before you place someone on floating status</h4>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Before placing an employee on floating status, ask:</p>

    <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
        <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Is there a genuine and temporary lack of work or assignment?</strong> Or is the employee actually being phased out?</li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Can the business explain why there is currently no available post?</strong></li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Are you genuinely looking for a new assignment?</strong></li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Can you document those efforts?</strong></li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Are you communicating appropriately with the employee?</strong></li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Have you recorded the date the floating status began?</strong></li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Have you calendared the six-month limit?</strong></li>
        <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>What will you do if the six-month period is approaching and there is still no assignment?</strong></li>
    </ul>

    <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The bottom line</h4>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Floating status is not inherently unlawful. In industries where assignments depend on client contracts, there can be legitimate periods when an employee temporarily has no post.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">But floating status is also not a loophole. The employer must have a legitimate basis for the temporary displacement, exercise the arrangement in good faith, and be able to substantiate the circumstances supporting it.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Most importantly, <strong>six months is a ceiling — not a safe harbor.</strong> The fact that an employer can temporarily keep an employee without an assignment does not mean it has an automatic right to do so for six months. And once the permissible period is approaching its end, the employer needs to decide whether the employee can be recalled or reassigned or whether a lawful separation based on an applicable authorized cause is necessary.</p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;"><strong>Floating status is temporary by design. The six-month rule is not a loophole; it is a boundary.</strong></p>
