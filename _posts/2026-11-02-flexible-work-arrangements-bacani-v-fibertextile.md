    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">When Work Days Get Cut: What Employers and Employees Should Know About Flexible Work Arrangements</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on November 2, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Imagine going to work six days a week for years. Then one day, your employer announces that you will now work only two or three days a week, and your pay shrinks right along with your schedule. No one asked for your opinion. A memo simply appears on the bulletin board. Is that allowed?</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The Supreme Court recently answered this question in <em>Bacani, et al. v. Fiber Textile Manufacturing Corp.</em> (G.R. No. 271518, September 30, 2025), a decision of the Court En Banc. Its answer is a reminder to every employer, and a source of reassurance to every worker: businesses may adjust work schedules in hard times, but only if they follow the rules.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Happened in the Case</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The workers in this case were employed by a textile manufacturer and were originally working six days a week. In July 2018, the company issued a memo reducing the schedule of its production workers to two to three days a week, on a rotating basis. The company explained that it had been locked out of its Valenzuela warehouse because of a property dispute, so raw materials were stuck inside and the factory ran short of work.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The workers sought help from the Department of Labor and Employment (DOLE). Shortly after, they were allegedly told by a company officer to look for other jobs and not to come back. They filed a complaint for constructive dismissal, which simply means being forced out of your job, even without a formal termination letter, because your work conditions or pay were made unbearable or unreasonable.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The Labor Arbiter sided with the workers. The National Labor Relations Commission (NLRC) and the Court of Appeals sided with the company. The Supreme Court reversed them and ruled that the workers were constructively dismissed.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Not Every "Flexible" Schedule Is the Same</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">One of the most useful parts of the decision is the way the Court separated two very different kinds of flexible work:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Flexible setups that employers and employees voluntarily agree on as ordinary business practice, with no cut in pay or benefits.</strong> Work-from-home and hybrid arrangements belong here. They are not rescue measures for a struggling business, and they do not shrink anyone's paycheck.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Flexible setups that reduce employees' pay or benefits so the business can cope with economic difficulty.</strong> These are the ones covered by DOLE Department Advisory No. 2, Series of 2009 (the "Advisory").</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">In plain terms, the Advisory is about <em>belt-tightening</em>, not <em>convenience</em>. Because work-from-home and hybrid arrangements are not meant to solve a company's financial troubles and do not reduce what workers take home, they do not fall under the Advisory. The Court did not decide the general wisdom or legality of work-from-home and hybrid arrangements in this case. Its ruling concerned arrangements that reduce employees' pay or benefits to address economic difficulties. In applying this distinction, employers should look at an arrangement's actual terms and purpose, and not rely only on what it is called.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Does the Advisory Cover?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Advisory lists several examples of flexible work arrangements. The list is not exhaustive, and other similar schemes meant to soften the impact of lost income are also covered:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Compressed workweek:</strong> fewer workdays per week, with longer working hours on the remaining days (not more than twelve hours a day), without reducing the total weekly hours. The Advisory describes a 48-hour week, but it recognizes that this can be adjusted for companies that normally operate on a five-day, 40-hour workweek.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Reduction of workdays:</strong> fewer workdays per week, which should not last more than six months.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Rotation of workers:</strong> employees take turns being given work within the week.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Forced leave:</strong> employees are required to go on leave for several days or weeks, using their leave credits if they have any.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Broken-time schedule:</strong> the workday is not continuous, but the total work hours remain the same.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Flexi-holidays:</strong> employees agree to take holidays on other days, as long as no existing benefits are lost.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">In this case, the company's schedule cut was a combination of reduced workdays and rotation of workers.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Four Requirements for a Valid Arrangement</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Based on the Advisory, the Court said an employer must satisfy all of the following:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>1. Real consent.</strong> The arrangement must be expressly and voluntarily supported by a majority of the affected workers, which means the employer must consult them <em>before</em> implementing it. The Court put it simply: informing the workers is one thing; securing their consent is another. The burden of proving consent rests on the employer.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>It must be temporary.</strong> These arrangements are meant to be short-term fixes. A reduction of workdays should not last more than six months.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Prior notice to DOLE.</strong> Before the arrangement starts, the employer must notify the DOLE Regional Office that covers the workplace, using the prescribed Report Form. The Advisory then provides that the Regional Office shall conduct an ocular visit to check that the arrangement complies with its rules.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A genuine business difficulty, handled in good faith.</strong> The employer must be suffering from actual or reasonably imminent economic difficulties (or a national emergency), and must show it with proof. The Court explained that the business does not have to be already losing money, but the difficulty must be real and the measure must be a good-faith response to it.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">These requirements concern the temporary flexible work arrangements covered by the Advisory, particularly those adopted to address economic difficulties or national emergencies. Not every change in work location or schedule necessarily falls under the same framework. Employers should look at the substance and purpose of the arrangement, its effect on employees' pay and benefits, and the legal requirements that apply.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The Advisory also requires employers to keep documents proving that the arrangement was voluntarily accepted by the workers. In short: <strong>if it is not written down and documented, it will be very hard to prove.</strong></p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Happened to the Employer in This Case?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court found that the company met only one requirement, which was that the arrangement was temporary. The normal schedule returned by January 2019. But it failed the other three:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>No proof of consent.</strong> The company only showed that it called a meeting and then posted a memo. It could not show that the workers were actually consulted or that most of them agreed.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>No notice to DOLE.</strong> The company presented no proof of notice and offered no explanation for skipping it.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>No proof of economic difficulty.</strong> The company submitted court pleadings from its property dispute, but these only contained allegations. It did not present financial reports or similar documents. The Court also noted that a shortage of raw materials does not automatically mean a business is in serious economic trouble, especially when the company could order new supplies and eventually resolve the problem.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Because the arrangement was invalid and the workers' pay was reduced, the Court ruled that they were constructively dismissed.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What If the Employer Only Forgets to Notify DOLE?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This was a new question for the Court, and its answer is important. The Advisory uses the word "shall" when it requires prior notice to DOLE, which makes the notice mandatory. But the Advisory does not say what happens if the notice is skipped.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court decided on a balanced rule:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>If the only thing missing is the notice to DOLE</strong>, and the arrangement was otherwise valid, the arrangement stays valid. However, the employer must pay <strong>PHP 100,000.00 in nominal damages to each affected employee</strong>. Nominal damages are awarded to recognize that a legal right was violated, even when the employee has not proven a specific amount of actual loss. Here, the Court set the amount at PHP 100,000.00 per affected employee for this particular failure.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>If the notice is missing and the other requirements are also not met</strong>, the arrangement is declared invalid, and the affected employees are entitled to the remedies for constructive or illegal dismissal.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The Court set the amount at PHP 100,000.00 (higher than the PHP 50,000.00 used in an older, similar ruling) to account for the loss in the peso's buying power over the last twenty years, and to make sure employers take the notice requirement seriously.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Consequences of Constructive Dismissal</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">For the seven workers whose complaints were upheld, the Court ordered the company to pay:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Full backwages</strong>, including allowances and other benefits, from the time of dismissal until the decision became final;</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Separation pay</strong> of one month's pay for every year of service (a fraction of at least six months counts as one year), in place of reinstatement because the relationship between the parties had already become strained;</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Proportionate 13th month pay and service incentive leave pay</strong>;</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Attorney's fees</strong> equal to 10% of the total award; and</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Legal interest</strong> of 6% per year on the total amount from the time the decision became final until fully paid.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Two other workers in the same case did not receive these awards because they failed to file their position papers. It is a quiet but important reminder that following procedure matters for employees too.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Two More Lessons From the Decision</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>First, no presumption of validity.</strong> The Court explained that flexible work arrangements of the kind covered by the Advisory, which reduce workers' pay or benefits as temporary measures against economic difficulties or national emergencies, are presumed illegal until the employer proves otherwise. They are accepted as valid only because, in exceptional circumstances, they can be a more humane option than layoffs or closing the business. In the Court's words, they are temporary "band-aid" solutions meant to help a business recover.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;"><strong>Second, asking for help is not abandoning your job.</strong> The company argued that the workers had simply stopped reporting for work. The Court disagreed. Workers who complain about their dismissal and seek help from DOLE are showing that they want to keep their jobs, which is the opposite of abandonment.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Practical Reminders for Employers</h4>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Talk to your employees first, and document their agreement. A memo or a bulletin board announcement is not consent.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Notify the DOLE Regional Office before the new schedule begins, using the required Report Form.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Gather and keep proof of your business difficulty, such as financial reports, production and inventory records, order cancellations, supply interruptions, and similar documents. Court pleadings alone will not be enough; the records should show how the problem actually affected the business and why a temporary schedule change was reasonably necessary.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Set a clear end date, and return to the regular schedule once things improve. For reduced workdays, do not go beyond six months.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Keep all records of consultation and consent. The Advisory requires it.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Practical Reminders for Employees</h4>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">If your schedule or pay is being reduced, ask whether there was a consultation and whether you are being asked to agree. You are not required to sign something you do not understand.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Keep copies of memos, schedules, payslips, and messages about the change.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">If you believe the arrangement is unfair, you may seek free assistance from the nearest DOLE office through the Single-Entry Approach (SEnA), or consult a lawyer.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">If you decide to file a complaint, comply with the deadlines and requirements, including submitting your position paper.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A Final Word</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Nothing in this decision says that employers can never adjust work schedules during tough times. The Court recognized that doing so can be a kinder alternative to layoffs or shutting down. What the Court insists on is fairness: consult the workers, be transparent with DOLE, back up your reasons with proof, and keep the arrangement temporary.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">When businesses and workers handle hard times together, openly and honestly, jobs can be saved and trust can be kept. That is the kind of flexibility the law supports.</p>
