    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Can HR Still Issue a Notice to Explain for Something That Happened a Year Ago?</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on October 28, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Picture this: HR is going through old records, or a complaint comes in, and the company learns about an incident that happened a year ago. Management wants to issue a Notice to Explain (NTE) to the employee involved. Is it too late?</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Not necessarily. Philippine labor law does not set one fixed deadline for issuing an NTE. But that does not mean the employer can take all the time it wants. The longer the wait, the more questions it will have to answer.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">First, a Quick Refresher: The Two-Notice Rule</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Before an employer can dismiss an employee for misconduct, it must follow a fair process with three parts:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The first notice (the NTE).</strong> It must tell the story of what the employee is accused of doing, with enough detail to understand the charge, and point to the company rule or ground involved. A general description is not enough. The employee must be given at least five calendar days, counted from receipt of the notice, to prepare a written reply.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A real chance to be heard.</strong> The employer should give the employee a hearing or conference to explain their side, present evidence, and answer the evidence against them. It does not have to be a courtroom-style trial, but it must be a genuine opportunity, not a formality.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The second notice.</strong> The employer tells the employee its decision and the reasons for it, in writing, after considering the explanation and the evidence.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Lighter penalties, like a warning or a short suspension, may also be governed by the company handbook or a union agreement (CBA). It is always worth checking what those documents say.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">So, Is There a Deadline for Issuing an NTE?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">For ordinary workplace discipline in the private sector, neither the Labor Code nor the DOLE rules say how many days an employer has to issue an NTE after an incident. The law is silent on this.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">But silence does not mean any delay is fine. Tribunals look at the whole picture, and these are the questions that usually matter:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>When did management learn about it?</strong> An employer who found out only during a recent audit is in a different position from one who knew all along.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>What did it do after finding out?</strong> Checking records and gathering facts can explain some delay.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Do company rules or a CBA set a time limit?</strong> If so, the employer must follow it.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Is the delay hurting fairness?</strong> Lost records, missing witnesses and fading memories make it harder for the employee to defend themselves, and harder for the employer to prove its case.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The three-year deadlines you may have heard of in the Labor Code, such as those for money claims, do not automatically apply to a company's own NTE.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Does Silence Mean the Offense Was Forgiven?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Not automatically. An employee may argue that management knew about the incident, did nothing for a long time, and therefore let it go. Whether that argument works depends on the evidence. The Supreme Court has said that a delay in disciplining someone does not, by itself, prove the employer gave up its right to do so. An employer's power to discipline is not lost by implication, and the employee must show real proof that management chose to forgive the offense.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Still, facts like these can strengthen the employee's side: the employee kept working normally after the incident, received good evaluations, or was even promoted. They do not settle the matter alone, but they will be weighed with everything else.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What If the Incident Happened a Year Ago?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A one-year gap does not, by itself, bar the employer from issuing an NTE. But if management knew about the incident from the start, the delay may matter when a tribunal looks at the evidence, the penalty, and any claim that the offense was forgiven. Here is why:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The employer carries the burden.</strong> If the employee is dismissed, it is the employer who must prove the dismissal was valid and that it followed the two-notice process. Old evidence is harder to prove with.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The delay needs an explanation.</strong> The employer should be ready to say when it found out, what it did next, and why it took this long.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The penalty must still fit.</strong> Even if the offense is proven, a tribunal may question whether a harsh penalty is fair in light of the delay and the surrounding facts.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>No double punishment.</strong> If the same offense was already penalized, the employer generally cannot punish it again. However, a past offense that was already penalized can still be considered when deciding the penalty for a new offense.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">When Can a Late NTE Still Hold Up?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A late NTE is easier to defend when:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">The incident was discovered only recently, through an audit, a complaint, or newly found records.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">The employer needed real time to verify the facts, and can show what it did.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">The employer can document when it learned of the incident and why it could not act sooner.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">The employee can still meaningfully answer the charge.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">None of this guarantees success. The employer must still prove the misconduct and follow the proper process.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Practical Tips for Employers and HR</h4>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Act promptly once management has enough information to start looking into the matter.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Write down when the incident happened, when you learned of it, and how.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">If checking the facts takes time, keep a record of the steps taken and why.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Make the NTE specific, and give at least five calendar days to reply.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Check your handbook and CBA for their own timelines.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Find out whether the same offense was already handled before.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Practical Tips for Employees</h4>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Do not ignore an NTE just because the incident is old. Reply within the given period and keep proof that you submitted it.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">If the delay hurts your defense, say so. Point out missing records or witnesses who are no longer around.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Keep copies of the NTE, your reply, and any evaluations or commendations you received afterward.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">If the same offense was already penalized, state that and attach proof.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">If you are suspended or dismissed, talk to a labor lawyer or contact DOLE or the NLRC promptly.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Bottom Line</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An employer can still issue an NTE for something that happened a year ago, and delay alone does not automatically cancel the case. But the absence of a deadline is not permission to wait forever.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">What matters is not just how much time has passed, but whether the employer can explain the delay, prove its case, and treat the employee fairly. In discipline, timeliness is part of fairness.</p>
