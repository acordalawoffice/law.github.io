    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Can an Employer Write “Terminated for Cause” in Your Certificate of Employment?</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on October 22, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Picture this. A dismissed employee asks for a Certificate of Employment (COE), and HR hands over a page that says, in black and white, “terminated for cause.” The employee is shocked. The employer shrugs and says, “But it is the truth.”</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">So who is right? The honest answer is a little more nuanced than a simple yes or no, so let us walk through it.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What a COE is really for</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A COE is proof that you worked for a company. Under DOLE Labor Advisory No. 06, Series of 2020, it specifies the dates of your employment and the kind of work you did. An employee who is still employed may ask for one too.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The employer must issue it within three days from your request. That right does not disappear because you resigned or were dismissed, and the employer should not hold the COE hostage to clearance, a signed waiver, or any other unrelated requirement.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Can the employer add the reason for separation?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Here is the important point: the advisory does not expressly forbid it, and it does not require it either. It tells the employer what a COE is and when to release it. It does not say that nothing else may be added, and it sets no penalty for a truthful extra statement.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">So it would be wrong to say that every COE containing the words “terminated for cause” automatically breaks a DOLE rule. But it would also be a mistake to think the employer can write anything it likes without consequences. “Not forbidden” is only the first question. The next one is whether the employer is taking on risk it does not need.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Where the real risks are</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Whether a particular COE entry leads to liability depends on the facts: the exact words, why they were used, and how the document was shared. Still, a few risks are worth knowing.</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The dismissal may later be ruled illegal.</strong> If that happens, the phrase “terminated for cause” becomes a statement the employer could not support. A ruling of illegal dismissal does not automatically mean the employer owes damages for the COE entry, but it makes the entry much harder to defend, and the employee may raise it as part of the dispute.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Fair dealing matters.</strong> The Civil Code requires everyone, employers included, to act with justice and good faith. A right used in bad faith, or in a way meant to hurt someone, can lead to a claim for damages. An unfavorable entry alone does not prove that, but a harsh, one-line label that the employee never asked for can invite that argument.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Defamation is possible, though not automatic.</strong> A statement that damages someone’s reputation is not libel by itself. It generally has to be communicated to another person, and truth together with good motive is an important defense. Handing the COE to the employee alone is not the same as spreading it to others, and if the employee later shows it to someone, that does not automatically make the employer responsible for libel. But a COE is meant to be shown, so an entry that is untrue or unfair can still lead to other claims, such as one for acting in bad faith under the Civil Code. Whether it does depends on the facts and the proof.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In short, the employer gains very little by adding the label, and may take on a good deal of risk.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">When saying more makes sense</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Adding details is not always a bad idea. It makes sense when the employee asks for it or it clearly works for the employee. For example:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>For a visa, loan, or job application,</strong> the employee may need the COE to show the last salary or position held.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>When the separation was not the employee’s fault,</strong> the COE may say “separated due to redundancy” or “employment ended upon completion of the project,” which helps the employee explain the gap to a new employer.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>For a resignation in good standing,</strong> the employee may want a line saying so.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>When a new employer or agency requires a stated reason,</strong> and the employee asks the former employer to provide it.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>As part of an agreed settlement,</strong> where both sides agree on the exact wording of the COE.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Notice the pattern: in each case, the extra detail is requested by the employee or agreed with them. When it is volunteered by the employer, and the news is bad, that is where the trouble begins.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Why employees rarely ask for the reason</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The soundest footing for stating the reason is that the employee asked for it or agreed to it. And honestly, who would ask?</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Employees usually want the reason on record when it works in their favor, such as a company closure, redundancy, the end of a project, or a resignation in good standing. For a dismissal for cause, an employee almost never asks for it to be written down. Without that request, the employer has no real reason to volunteer it, and it carries the risks described above.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A COE is not a job reference</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">These are two different things. The COE is a short record of service that goes to the employee. A reference is what a former employer says when a new employer calls to ask about a candidate.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An employer who gets a reference call may answer truthfully, but that does not mean it can say everything it knows. What it shares should be accurate, documented, relevant to what the new employer needs to know, and given in good faith. Because this involves personal information, the Data Privacy Act of 2012 may also apply, so it is wise to be careful about what is disclosed and to whom.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Practical tips for employers</h4>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Issue the COE on time.</strong> Release it within three days from the request.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Do not tie it to clearance.</strong> Deal with unreturned items or unfinished accountabilities separately.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Keep the details in the right place.</strong> Notices, investigation reports, and termination papers belong in the employee’s personnel file, not on the COE.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Make neutral the default.</strong> Unless there is a good reason to say more, stick to dates of employment and the work performed.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>If the reason is stated, get the employee’s request in writing.</strong> This is good practice, not something the advisory expressly requires. Use specific, factual words rather than a one-line label.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Handle reference calls with care.</strong> Share only what is accurate and supportable, and think about privacy before disclosing.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A word to employees</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">You may request a COE no matter how your employment ended. If it is delayed, refused, or made conditional on something unrelated, you may ask the nearest DOLE office for help.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If your COE contains a statement you believe is untrue or unfair, keep a copy and talk to a lawyer before deciding what to do. What you can do depends on the exact wording, how it was used, and the evidence you have.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The bottom line</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">“Allowed” and “wise” are not the same thing. The DOLE advisory does not expressly bar an employer from stating the reason for separation, but doing so without being asked invites more problems than it solves.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">For employers, a neutral COE is usually the better choice. Keep it simple, factual, and on time, and save the details of any disciplinary matter for the records where they belong.</p>
