    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Is Your Truck Driver Really 'Field Personnel'? What GPS Can (and Can't) Prove</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on October 03, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The truck leaves the warehouse at 6:00 in the morning. The dispatcher knows the route. GPS shows exactly where the truck is. The delivery app records every stop, and the customer signs on a phone screen with the time stamped right there. By the end of the day, the company can pretty much replay the whole trip.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">So here is a question worth asking: can that same company still say the driver is &ldquo;field personnel&rdquo;, meaning an employee whose actual working hours cannot be determined with reasonable certainty?</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The short answer is that it depends, and the Supreme Court has already told us what it depends on.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Working outside the office is not the same as being &ldquo;field personnel&rdquo;</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Many people assume that a driver is field personnel simply because he spends the day on the road. That is not what the law says. Article 82 of the Labor Code describes field personnel as non-agricultural employees who (1) regularly do their work away from the employer's main office or branch, <em>and</em> (2) whose actual hours of work in the field cannot be determined with reasonable certainty.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Both parts must be present, and the second part is the one people forget. In <em>Auto Bus Transport Systems, Inc. v. Bautista</em> (G.R. No. 156367, May 16, 2005), the Supreme Court explained that it is not only about where the employee works. It is also about whether the employer supervises his time and performance.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">So the better question is not &ldquo;Does he work outside the office?&rdquo; It is &ldquo;Can the company reasonably tell when he is actually working, and does it actually supervise how he does it?&rdquo;</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Why the label matters</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Genuine field personnel fall outside the Labor Code's rules on hours of work and rest periods (Book Three, Title I). Because they are excluded from that part of the Code, they generally do not receive the benefits found there: overtime pay, night shift differential, premium pay for rest days and holidays, holiday pay and service incentive leave. That is not a special rule against field personnel. It is simply the result of Article 82 placing them outside Title I's coverage. For a company with a large fleet, that is a big deal for payroll.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">But it is not a free pass to skip every benefit. Field personnel are still entitled to at least the minimum wage, are still protected against illegal dismissal, and are still covered by the government-mandated contributions. And 13th month pay is governed separately by P.D. No. 851 and its own coverage rules. It does not disappear just because someone is classified as field personnel.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">When the Court said &ldquo;not field personnel&rdquo;</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court has dealt with drivers and delivery workers more than once. In these three cases, the employees were not field personnel:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><em>Far East Agricultural Supply, Inc. v. Lebatique</em> (G.R. No. 162813, February 12, 2007): A company truck driver was told to deliver goods at specific times and places, had no say in choosing or approaching customers, and the company even directed drivers to stay at the client's premises during truck-ban hours.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><em>Dasco v. Philtranco Service Enterprises, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 211141, June 29, 2016): Bus drivers and conductors had to be at terminals at set times, and dispatchers and checkers along the route supervised their time and performance.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><em>Marby Food Ventures Corporation v. Dela Cruz</em> (G.R. No. 244629, July 28, 2020): Delivery employees were directed to make deliveries at specified times and places and had to log their time-in and time-out, so their actual hours could be determined with reasonable certainty. They were awarded overtime pay, holiday pay and service incentive leave pay.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In each case, the Court did not stop at &ldquo;he drives a truck.&rdquo; It looked at how the company actually ran its operations.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Meet Driver A and Driver B</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Driver A is handed a truck and told to make his deliveries. Beyond general instructions, he largely decides when to leave, where to stop, how long to stay and when to return. The company has little reliable information from which to reconstruct his actual working time.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Driver B gets an electronic dispatch, follows an assigned route in a GPS-equipped truck, keeps to a delivery schedule, updates dispatch along the way, records each delivery electronically, and returns the truck to a designated facility.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Both work &ldquo;in the field.&rdquo; But the company can probably rebuild most of Driver B's day from its own records, and that is a very different situation from Driver A's. Location alone cannot settle the question.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Then came GPS</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Modern fleet systems can capture GPS location, departure and arrival times, route history, electronic dispatch records, electronic proof of delivery, customer time stamps and messages with dispatch. Article 82 has not changed, but the facts it is applied to have changed a lot.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">It is tempting to say: &ldquo;If we can see the truck, we can determine the hours, so the driver is not field personnel.&rdquo; But the Supreme Court has already considered that argument, and it did not accept it.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">It cuts both ways: the <em>Hisoler</em> case</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In <em>Hisoler III v. Filipino Travel Center Corporation</em> (G.R. No. 232949, resolution dated November 20, 2017), a driver who had been dismissed for allowing other persons to ride along with paying customers also argued that he was not field personnel. With modern technology such as GPS and a cellular phone, he said, his employer could monitor his hours and performance. The Court was not persuaded. It acknowledged that the company could follow the progress and location of his trip through the phone and the vehicle's GPS.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Even so, the Court agreed with the labor tribunals and the Court of Appeals that he was field personnel. Why? Because knowing where the vehicle is does not mean supervising what the driver does. As the labor tribunal observed, he was able to let unauthorized persons tour without the company&rsquo;s knowledge, and the Court of Appeals noted that the hours he spent on the actual field work were left to his own control. In other words, his performance remained unsupervised. To be fair, <em>Hisoler</em> is a short resolution denying a petition, not a lengthy decision, so it is best read as an illustration of how the Court looks at these facts.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">So the lesson is not &ldquo;GPS means overtime,&rdquo; and it is not &ldquo;GPS means nothing&rdquo; either. The better question is this: <strong>the GPS may know where the truck is, but does the company actually know, and supervise, what the employee is doing?</strong></p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Read <em>Hisoler</em> together with <em>Dasco</em> and <em>Marby</em>, and an important distinction emerges. Location tracking alone did not make the driver&rsquo;s hours determinable in <em>Hisoler</em>. But in <em>Dasco</em> and <em>Marby</em>, the companies also had dispatchers and checkers, fixed schedules and required time logs. Those were the facts that showed the drivers&rsquo; work was supervised and their hours reasonably determinable. The Court is not applying a formula here. It is looking at the facts of each case.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">That is why a company with GPS, electronic dispatch, fixed delivery schedules, required check-ins and electronic delivery records may have a much harder time saying that a driver&rsquo;s hours cannot be determined with reasonable certainty than a company that simply has a GPS dot on a screen. And it matters how the company actually uses its technology. Software that nobody looks at is very different from a dispatch team that follows trips in real time.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">It also means some drivers really are field personnel. Those who set their own schedules, have little day-to-day oversight and cannot be reliably tracked can fall under Article 82, even in the age of GPS.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">GPS does not automatically mean overtime, either</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">GPS tells you where the truck is. It does not tell you what the driver was doing every minute. A stopped truck could mean a delivery, waiting to load or unload, heavy traffic, a meal break or an emergency on the road.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">What counts as working time is a separate question. Under Article 84 of the Labor Code, hours worked include time an employee is required to be on duty or at a prescribed workplace, as well as time the employee is allowed or permitted to work. Short rest periods during working hours also count as hours worked. Whether waiting time is compensable depends on the circumstances, such as whether the employee is required to wait or whether the waiting is an integral part of the job. So not every stop that shows up on a GPS map is working time.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Not every logistics employee is in the same boat</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A freight-forwarding company may have truck drivers, delivery coordinators, field representatives, warehouse staff, customs liaisons and office-based documentation staff. Their working arrangements can be completely different. A warehouse worker on a fixed shift is not field personnel just because the company is in logistics, and neither is an office-based documentation staffer. &ldquo;Logistics employee&rdquo; is not a category under Article 82.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The cost of getting it wrong</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Who has to prove what depends on the benefit being claimed. For overtime, the employee must first show that he actually worked beyond the regular eight hours, although the company&rsquo;s own admission that he did can settle that point. For holiday pay and service incentive leave, the employer generally has to prove payment or compliance, because the payrolls and personnel records are in its hands.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">And when a company answers a claim by saying the employee is field personnel, it needs facts to support that classification. A job title, a contract, or the fact that the employee drives a truck is not enough. The actual working arrangement has to fit Article 82.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">That is where the company&rsquo;s own records come in. GPS logs, dispatch records, delivery time stamps, trip reports, gate records and messages with dispatch can end up as evidence for or against the company&rsquo;s position. The technology does not decide the issue by itself. The records help show how the work was actually done and supervised.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Money claims can also reach back three years (Article 306). In <em>Marby</em>, the Court limited the award to the three years before the complaint was filed. Now imagine that across a fleet of drivers.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A quick checklist for HR</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If your company treats drivers or other mobile employees as field personnel, ask yourself:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Does the company set the start time, the route and the destinations?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Are delivery schedules fixed, or do drivers decide their own timing?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Do dispatchers, GPS or mobile apps actually monitor trips, and does the company really use them?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Are arrival and departure times recorded through proof of delivery, customer time stamps or gate logs?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Must drivers report delays, stops and route changes?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Does the company only know where the vehicle is, or does it also supervise how the work is done?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Does the classification on paper match how the work is really done? A label in a contract does not change the actual arrangement.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">One more practical note: location information linked to an identifiable employee can constitute personal information under the Data Privacy Act. Employers using GPS or telematics should therefore be transparent about what data is collected, the purposes for which it is used, and the safeguards that protect it.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The bottom line</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Technology does not change Article 82. It changes the evidence available to determine whether Article 82 actually applies.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">So, can you still say you can&rsquo;t track the hours? A company may be able to track the truck without being able to show that it supervised the driver&rsquo;s work, and that was enough for the Court in <em>Hisoler</em>. But when GPS, dispatch, schedules, check-ins and electronic delivery records work together, those records may provide substantial evidence from which the employee&rsquo;s actual working time can be determined. In that situation, a company may have a much harder time establishing that the hours cannot be determined with reasonable certainty.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The better question is not &ldquo;Is our driver field personnel?&rdquo; It is &ldquo;Based on how we actually operate, can we honestly say so?&rdquo; It is far better to answer that before it turns into an overtime claim.</p>
