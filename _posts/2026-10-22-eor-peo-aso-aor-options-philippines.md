    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Beyond the EOR: How Foreign Companies Can Hire in the Philippines</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on October 22, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Want to hire Filipino talent without setting up a Philippine company right away? An Employer of Record (EOR) is one way to do it, but it is far from the only one. Foreign businesses can also look at a PEO, an ASO, an AOR, independent contractors, outsourcing providers, staffing agencies, or their own Philippine entity.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Here is the catch: these labels are not used consistently. Two providers that both call themselves an "EOR" or a "PEO" may offer very different arrangements. So instead of relying on the label, let us look at what each option really involves and what the law cares about.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The One Test Behind Every Option</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Whatever structure you choose, Philippine law looks at the real relationship, not the wording of a contract. Who engages and pays the workers? Who controls how the work is done? Who can discipline or dismiss them? And if a middle company is involved, is it a genuine independent business?</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Philippine rules on contracting allow legitimate arrangements but prohibit "labor-only contracting," which is essentially a middle company that is a name on paper while the client does the real directing. If that is what the facts show, the client can be treated as the workers' employer.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Two things are worth remembering. First, <strong>legitimate contracting does not mean zero liability.</strong> Even when the contractor is genuine, the Labor Code (Arts. 106-109) can make the client answerable together with the contractor for unpaid wages and other violations of the Code, to the extent the law provides. Second, registration alone does not guarantee that an arrangement is legitimate, and the mere fact that workers do work related to your business does not, by itself, make it labor-only contracting. The actual facts decide.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">With that in mind, here are your options.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Option 1: Your Own Philippine Company</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If you are planning a long-term operation, setting up your own local presence is the most direct route. The common forms are:</p>

        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A subsidiary.</strong> A separate Philippine company that you own. It can do business and employ workers directly.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A branch office.</strong> An extension of your foreign company, not a separate one. Your parent company generally remains responsible for what the branch does.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A representative office.</strong> Meant for liaison and promotion, not for earning income in the Philippines, so it does not suit a team doing revenue-generating work.</li>
        </ul>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;"><strong>Best for:</strong> sustained operations, a core local team, and businesses that want direct control. <strong>Keep in mind:</strong> the right form depends on your activities, industry, foreign-ownership limits, capital, registrations and tax position. Check the current requirements before choosing.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Option 2: Employer of Record (EOR)</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A Philippine company formally employs the workers and handles payroll and statutory contributions, while you receive their services. It can be a practical way to build a team before deciding whether to set up your own entity. But the label does not prove the arrangement is lawful, so check that the provider genuinely performs the employer's role and that daily practice matches the contract.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;"><strong>Plan the exit from day one.</strong> If you may set up your own company later, agree now on how the team will move: continuity of service, accrued benefits, employee consent where needed, and who bears the costs. Moving workers to a new entity is not a simple admin step.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Option 3: PEO (Professional Employer Organization)</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A PEO is commonly associated with "co-employment," a model in which the provider and the client share certain employer responsibilities. This arrangement is familiar in the United States. In the Philippines, however, calling a provider a "PEO" does not automatically establish a legally recognized co-employment relationship. Some providers primarily offer HR and administrative support to companies that already have a Philippine entity, while others offer arrangements in which they employ workers assigned to client companies. The legal consequences depend on the actual arrangement, the parties' respective roles, and the applicable Philippine labor laws.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;"><strong>Ask the provider:</strong> "Who actually employs my workers, who handles the employer's responsibilities, and how does your model work under Philippine law?" If the answer is vague, that tells you something.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Option 4: ASO (Administrative Services Organization) and Payroll Providers</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An ASO handles the paperwork: payroll, HR administration, benefits and records. It does not employ anyone. If your Philippine company employs the workers, <strong>you</strong> remain the employer, and outsourcing the admin does not pass your legal responsibilities to the provider.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;"><strong>Best for:</strong> companies with a Philippine entity but no full HR team. Review the contract for accuracy, data privacy, service levels and who pays for errors. Even if the provider is contractually responsible, you are still answerable to your employees and the government.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Option 5: AOR (Agent or Contractor of Record) and Independent Contractors</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An AOR helps you engage independent contractors by handling agreements, invoices and payments. It does not employ them, and its involvement does not make someone an independent contractor.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Philippine law uses the four-fold test to check for employment: who selects and engages the worker, who pays wages, who can dismiss, and who controls how the work is done. Control is usually the most important. Having several clients, using your own laptop, working remotely or being paid per project does not settle the question by itself. A specialist can still be an employee if the real relationship looks like employment.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;"><strong>The warning:</strong> if someone is labeled a contractor but works under your schedule, instructions and supervision, the label and the AOR would not protect you. Also check the tax and registration side, especially if the provider can negotiate or sign contracts on your behalf.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Option 6: Outsourcing or Managed Services</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Here you are buying a result, such as customer support, software development or bookkeeping, not individual workers. A genuine provider manages its own people and answers for the outcome, while you set deliverables, deadlines and service levels. <strong>Best for:</strong> a whole function or project. It is not automatically low-risk, though. A registration and a "managed services" contract do not prove legitimacy, so look at the provider's real operations, capital and control over its own workforce.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Option 7: Staffing Agencies and Local Partners</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A <strong>staffing or manpower agency</strong> supplies workers, often short-term. The same contracting rules apply, so it must be a genuine, properly registered and capitalized business that truly controls its workers.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">A <strong>joint venture or local partner</strong> can make sense if your industry limits foreign ownership, or if you want local expertise and an existing workforce. It brings its own due diligence and control questions, so plan it carefully.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">So, Which One Should You Choose?</h4>

        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Testing the market with a small team:</strong> consider an EOR or a similar legitimate arrangement, with a clear plan for moving the team later.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Genuinely independent specialists:</strong> consider contractors, with an AOR if you want help with the paperwork, after confirming the real relationship supports it.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>An entire function with clear outputs:</strong> consider a genuine outsourcing provider.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A long-term core team:</strong> consider your own Philippine company, with an ASO or payroll provider if you need support.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Short-term extra hands:</strong> check any staffing agency against the contracting rules instead of relying on its assurances.</li>
        </ul>

                <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Keep the Roles Clear in Real Life</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Whichever option you choose, agree on what your managers can and cannot do. A short, written "direction protocol" works well. You can set goals, deadlines and quality standards, but hiring, pay, leave, discipline and dismissal should go through the real employer. The aim is not to stop you from running your business. It is to make sure the paperwork and the daily practice say the same thing.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Five Questions to Ask Before You Hire</h4>

        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>What are we actually engaging?</strong> Employees, genuinely independent contractors, or a provider that delivers a service?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Do we need a Philippine entity?</strong> Think about your activities, ownership, registration and tax position.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Who will manage the workers?</strong> Identify who directs the work and who makes employment decisions.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Does the setup comply with Philippine law?</strong> Review employment status, the contracting rules, statutory obligations and how things will really operate.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>What happens if it ends?</strong> Settle notice, transition, accrued obligations, data and costs in advance.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Bottom Line</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">You do not have to set up a Philippine company just to hire Filipino talent. But using an EOR, PEO, AOR or outsourcing provider does not automatically solve every employment-law problem either. The right choice depends on your goals, the kind of work, how much control you need, and what the provider actually does.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Before you sign, have the structure, the provider's contract and the way responsibilities will really be divided reviewed under Philippine law. Tax and registration questions may need a separate look. A setup that works commercially should also stand up legally.</p>
