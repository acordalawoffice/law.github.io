    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Valid Reason, No Notice: Is the Dismissal Illegal?</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on October 28, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Here is a situation that comes up more often than you might think. An employer has a real, legitimate reason to end someone's employment, maybe the employee committed a serious violation, or the company truly had to close a department. But in the rush to get it done, the employer skips the proper steps. No written notice. No chance for the employee to explain. Just a termination.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Does that make the dismissal illegal? Surprisingly, the answer is usually no. But it does not mean the employer gets off free. Let us walk through what the Supreme Court says, in plain language.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What is "due process" in a dismissal?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Due process simply means fairness. Before an employee loses a job, the law requires two kinds of fairness:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A fair reason (the "what").</strong> The employer must have a cause recognized by law. These fall into two groups: <em>just causes</em>, which are based on something the employee did (for example, serious misconduct, willful disobedience, or fraud), and <em>authorized causes</em>, which are based on the business or the employee's situation, not on any wrongdoing (for example, redundancy, retrenchment to prevent losses, closure of the business, or a disease that meets the strict requirements of the law, including a certification from a competent public health authority that it cannot be cured within six months despite proper treatment).</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A fair procedure (the "how").</strong> Even with a good reason, the employer must follow the proper steps. This is called <em>procedural due process</em>.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The steps depend on the type of cause:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>For a just cause:</strong> (1) a first written notice that spells out the specific acts being complained about, the ground for dismissal, and the company rule allegedly violated, if any, not just a general description, and that invites the employee to submit a written explanation; (2) a meaningful opportunity to answer the charges and present evidence, with at least five calendar days from receipt of the first notice to prepare a written reply; and (3) a second written notice stating the employer's decision and the reasons for it. A formal hearing or conference is not always required, since the employee may be heard in writing or in some other fair way. It becomes necessary when the employee asks for it in writing, when there are substantial disputes about the evidence, when company rules or practice call for it, or in similar circumstances. These requirements are explained in <em>King of Kings Transport, Inc. v. Mamac</em> (G.R. No. 166208, June 29, 2007), <em>Perez v. Philippine Telegraph and Telephone Company</em> (G.R. No. 152048, April 7, 2009), and DOLE Department Order No. 147-15, Series of 2015.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>For an authorized cause:</strong> a written notice to both the employee and the Department of Labor and Employment (DOLE) at least 30 days before the termination takes effect.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Why a missed step does not automatically make the dismissal illegal</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court went back and forth on this question over the years. It eventually settled the matter in the landmark case of <em>Agabon v. NLRC</em> (G.R. No. 158693, November 17, 2004). The employees there were dismissed for a valid reason, but the employer did not follow the proper notice procedure.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court's reasoning is straightforward. A dismissal is legal or illegal mainly because of <em>why</em> it happened. If the employer establishes a valid cause, the employee is not entitled to reinstatement or backwages merely because the employer failed to follow the required procedure. The procedural lapse is still a separate wrong, one that is the employer's responsibility, and the employer may be made to answer for it through nominal damages.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">So the rule today is this: if the cause is valid, the dismissal generally stands despite the procedural defect, but the employer may be held liable for nominal damages for violating the employee's right to statutory due process.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">So what are the consequences for the employer?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">When the employer proves a valid cause but fails to follow the required procedure, that defect alone does not invalidate the dismissal. The employee may be entitled to nominal damages for the violation of statutory due process. In that situation, the employer:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">does <strong>not</strong> have to reinstate the employee;</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">does <strong>not</strong> have to pay backwages;</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">may be ordered to pay <strong>nominal damages</strong>; and</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">must still pay separation pay where the law requires it for the particular authorized cause invoked. For example, redundancy and retrenchment require separation pay, while closure due to proven serious business losses generally does not (see DOLE Department Order No. 147-15). Whether separation pay is due, and how much, depends on the particular authorized cause and the law, not on whether the employer complied with the notice requirement.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What are nominal damages?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Under the Civil Code, nominal damages are not meant to make up for a financial loss. They are awarded to recognize that a person's right was violated, and to give that person some form of vindication. Think of it as the law's way of saying: "Your right to a fair process was ignored, and that was wrong."</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Because they are not tied to actual loss, the employee does not need to prove how much they suffered. In practice, the amount is not computed from the employee's salary or years of service. The Court fixes it based on the circumstances, including how serious the employer's violation was, with a clear purpose: to discourage employers from cutting corners in the future.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">How much must the employer pay? (Local employees)</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court has established two principal benchmarks, depending on whether the dismissal is based on a just cause or an authorized cause. The Court explained the difference this way: in a just cause case, the employee's own act set the process in motion, while in an authorized cause case, the employer's own business decision did:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><strong>&#8369;30,000 when the dismissal is for a just cause.</strong> Here, the employee's own act set things in motion, so the penalty is lighter. This was the amount in <em>Agabon v. NLRC</em> (2004), and it was applied again in cases such as <em>Aparece v. J. Marketing Corporation</em> (G.R. No. 174224, October 17, 2008), where the employer held an investigation but never gave the formal written notice of the charges. In <em>King of Kings Transport</em>, a bus conductor was validly dismissed for dishonesty, but the company never gave him a written charge sheet and held no hearing, so it paid &#8369;30,000.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><strong>&#8369;50,000 when the dismissal is for an authorized cause.</strong> Here, the employer decided to end the job as a business choice, not because of anything the employee did, so the penalty is stiffer. This was fixed in <em>Jaka Food Processing Corporation v. Pacot</em> (G.R. No. 151378, March 28, 2005) and followed in later cases like <em>Culili v. Eastern Telecommunications Philippines, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 165381, February 9, 2011), where an employee dismissed for redundancy without proper notice received &#8369;50,000 on top of his separation pay, and <em>Celebes Japan Foods Corporation v. Yermo</em> (G.R. No. 175855, October 2, 2009), where each affected employee was awarded &#8369;50,000.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Two more things worth knowing:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>The amount is awarded per employee.</strong> If ten employees were let go without proper notice, the employer may owe nominal damages to each of them.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>These figures are benchmarks, not fixed prices.</strong> They come from Supreme Court decisions, not from a law that sets an automatic penalty, so they do not apply the same way in every case. The Court has kept its discretion to look at the circumstances. In <em>De Jesus v. Aquino</em> (G.R. No. 164662, February 18, 2013), for example, the Court awarded &#8369;50,000 even though the dismissal was for a just cause. In <em>Celebes Japan Foods</em>, the employer asked the Court to reduce the award to &#8369;5,000 per employee because of its financial condition. The Court refused, explaining that the employer's finances should not be the only basis and that the seriousness of the violation matters, especially since there was no real attempt to give notice at all.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>A newer ruling suggests the numbers may not stay put.</strong> In <em>Bacani v. Fiber Textile Manufacturing Corp.</em> (G.R. No. 271518, September 30, 2025), the Court En Banc dealt with an employer that cut its workers' workdays without first notifying the DOLE, as the DOLE advisory on flexible work arrangements requires. For an otherwise valid arrangement with that missing notice, the Court fixed nominal damages at &#8369;100,000 per employee. It explained that the &#8369;50,000 set in <em>Jaka</em> in 2005 would take about &#8369;108,000 to match in 2025 because the peso has lost so much purchasing power. That ruling concerned work schedules, not dismissals, and it did not expressly change the &#8369;30,000 and &#8369;50,000 benchmarks for dismissals. A concurring opinion even noted that the amount for procedurally defective dismissals was still &#8369;30,000 as of 2025, while arguing that figures like it deserve adjustment for inflation. Still, employers should not assume those older figures will stay the same.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What about Overseas Filipino Workers (OFWs)?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The same principle applies to overseas workers, although the exact rules depend on the employment contract and the laws that govern it. Seafarers, for example, are generally covered by the POEA Standard Employment Contract, which has its own version of the fair process rule. Under that contract, a seafarer facing dismissal for a just cause is generally entitled to a written notice of the charges, a chance to explain during the prescribed investigation or hearing, and a written notice of the decision and its reasons, subject to any applicable exception. One such exception is this: if there is a clear and existing danger to the safety of the crew or the vessel, the master may dismiss without the usual notice, but must send a complete, well-supported report to the manning agency.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">When a seafarer is dismissed for a valid reason but not given a fair chance to explain, the Supreme Court has followed <em>Agabon</em>. In <em>Abosta Shipmanagement Corporation v. NLRC</em> (G.R. No. 163252, July 27, 2011), a radio officer was dismissed for a valid cause but was let go right away, without reasonable time to answer the charges. The Court upheld the dismissal but awarded &#8369;30,000 as nominal damages for the violation of his right to a fair process.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">One important caution for OFWs and their employers: nominal damages only come into play when there is a <em>valid cause</em> for the dismissal. The Supreme Court's 2023 decision in <em>I-People Manpower Resources, Inc. v. Court of Appeals</em> (G.R. No. 246410, January 25, 2023) shows what can happen otherwise.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An electrical engineer hired to work in Qatar was told that his two-year contract would end within 30 days because of "low activity" and a lack of projects. His contract allowed either side to end it by giving one month's written notice, with no reason required, and the employer argued that this was enough. The Court disagreed. A work contract must be read together with our labor laws, and parties cannot contract their way around them. Since the contract was made in the Philippines, Philippine law applied, which meant the employer still had to prove a valid authorized cause (here, retrenchment) with real evidence rather than bare statements, and give the proper written notices to both the employee and the DOLE. The employee's polite thank-you email to the company did not waive his right to question the dismissal.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The dismissal was declared illegal. The employer was ordered to pay the salaries for the unexpired portion of the contract, to reimburse the placement fees, and to pay attorney's fees, although the Court did not award moral or exemplary damages because there was no bad faith. In other words, skipping the process is costly, but having no proven valid reason at all is far more costly.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A quick way to tell the situations apart</h4>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Valid just cause, but the process was skipped:</strong> the dismissal generally stands, and nominal damages may be awarded.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Valid authorized cause, but the required notice was not given:</strong> the dismissal generally stands, and nominal damages may be awarded. Separation pay, if the law requires it, is a separate matter.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>No valid cause at all:</strong> the dismissal may be illegal, and the employee's remedies come from the illegal dismissal itself. Nominal damages are not simply added on top of those remedies. In <em>Coogee International Inc. v. Maningo</em> (G.R. No. 264881, July 16, 2025), for example, workers were let go on the claim that their project had ended, but the employer could not prove it. The Court deleted the &#8369;30,000 nominal damages the Court of Appeals had awarded, because nominal damages apply only when there is a valid cause and cannot co-exist with the actual damages (backwages and separation pay) the workers were already receiving.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The takeaway</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>For employers:</strong> having a good reason is only half the job. Take the time to give the notices, listen to the employee's side, and document everything. Doing so costs far less than the nominal damages, and it also protects you if the reason itself is ever questioned. Make sure the first notice clearly states the charge and that dismissal is being considered; earlier warning memos, or a notice that only asks for an explanation, may not be enough (<em>Aparece</em>; <em>De Jesus</em>), and telling the employee about the charges only verbally does not satisfy the written notice requirement (<em>King of Kings</em>). Remember, the burden of proving that a dismissal was valid rests on the employer.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>For employees:</strong> if you were let go without proper notice or a chance to explain, that matters, and the law gives you a remedy. But if the employer had a valid reason, expect the remedy to be nominal damages rather than getting your job back or receiving backwages.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Every dismissal has its own facts, and the amounts discussed above are benchmarks drawn from decided cases. If you are facing a termination, whether as an employer or an employee, it is worth getting advice early.</p>
