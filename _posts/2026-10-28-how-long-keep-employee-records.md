    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">How Long Should You Really Keep Your Employees' Records?</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on October 28, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Every HR office eventually asks the same question: can we finally throw out that box of old 201 files sitting in the storage room? It sounds like a simple housekeeping matter, but it is actually a legal one. Philippine employers are required to keep certain employment records for specific periods, and different records answer to different rules — some to the Labor Code, some to tax law, some to social legislation. Get the timing wrong, and you either destroy something you needed to defend a claim, or you hold on to sensitive personal data far longer than the law allows.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">This guide walks through the main categories of HR records, how long each one should realistically be kept, and the legal basis behind each rule — in plain language, without the legalese.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The General Rule: Three Years</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The baseline rule for most employment records is three years from the date of the last entry. This comes from the rules implementing the Labor Code, and it covers the records employers are generally required to keep and maintain — things like payroll ledgers and individual time records.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A quick but important distinction: this three-year rule is a recordkeeping requirement. It happens to line up with the three-year period an employee generally has to file a money claim, but the two rules come from different provisions and should not be treated as one and the same. In practice, though, most companies use three years as their internal floor for 201 files, employment contracts, personnel action forms, and leave records — not because each of these documents has its own separate three-year rule, but because it is a sensible, defensible minimum for the broader category of employment records.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Payroll and Tax Records: Ten Years (for the BIR Side)</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Payroll wears two hats. As an employment record, it falls under the three-year rule above. But once payroll data becomes part of your books of accounts or supports your tax filings — think withholding tax records and accounting entries — the Bureau of Internal Revenue's rules take over, and those require a much longer memory: ten years, reckoned from the day after the deadline for filing the related tax return (or the actual filing date, if filed late), for the taxable year when the last entry was made in the books. This 10-year baseline comes from Revenue Regulations No. 17-2013. A later amendment, Revenue Regulations No. 5-2014, added the two-stage format: for the first five years, hard copies are required; after that, an electronic copy may generally be retained instead, provided it is kept in an electronic storage system that meets the BIR's requirements.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">One practical warning here: a folder of PDFs on your desktop or a shared Google Drive does not automatically qualify as compliant electronic storage. The BIR's rules call for a proper electronic storage system with specific indexing, security, and integrity safeguards — not just any digital copy. If your setup does not meet those standards, the safer route is to keep the original hard copies for the full ten years rather than assume the digital version will pass muster.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">One more wrinkle: if a record is relevant to a pending tax protest or a refund or credit claim, it needs to be kept until that matter is fully resolved — regardless of whether the ten years have already run out.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Workplace Safety and Accident Records</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This is the category most companies get wrong — usually by citing a "five-year rule" that does not actually exist in the way people think. Here is the accurate picture.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Occupational Safety and Health Standards require employers to maintain an accident or illness record, open at all times for inspection, containing details like the date of the incident, the affected employee, the cause, and the extent of disability. What this rule does <em>not</em> do is specify a fixed number of years for keeping that record. The rule requires the employer to maintain and keep the record available for inspection; it does not, in that provision, prescribe a fixed number of years after which the record may be destroyed.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Separately, the Labor Code's employees' compensation provisions require a logbook chronologically recording sickness, injury, or death in the workplace, with entries made within five days of learning about the incident. That five-day figure is a reporting deadline, not a retention period — an easy mix-up, since both rules involve the number five in different ways.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">More recently, the Department of Labor and Employment issued Department Order No. 252-25, the revised implementing rules of the Occupational Safety and Health Law, which replaced the older Department Order No. 198-18. This newer order adds reporting obligations — like the Annual Medical Report, the Annual Work Accident/Illness Exposure Data Report, and the monthly Employer's Work Accident/Illness Report — but it does not appear to introduce a specific retention period for the underlying accident and illness records either.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The practical takeaway: because these rules do not prescribe a fixed expiration date for the underlying accident or illness record, the employer should set a reasonable retention period in its own records policy — one that takes into account the nature of the incident, applicable legal requirements, potential claims, and the company's legitimate need for the record. Records should be kept longer whenever an actual, pending, threatened, or reasonably foreseeable claim, inspection, investigation, or proceeding requires it.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">To put a number on that: a workplace injury can give rise to more than one type of claim, each running on its own clock. A claim for Employees' Compensation benefits before the SSS or GSIS must generally be filed within three years of the injury, illness, or death. A civil suit for damages based on negligence (a quasi-delict) has a four-year prescriptive period under the Civil Code, and Philippine jurisprudence allows an employee to pursue this route on top of EC benefits in cases involving employer fault. If the claim is instead framed as a breach of a written obligation, the prescriptive period can stretch to ten years. Because you would not necessarily know in advance which of these routes an injured employee might pursue, many company legal teams treat the accident record as worth keeping for at least three to ten years from the date of the incident, rather than leaving the timeline entirely open-ended.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Household Employers and Kasambahay Records</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If you employ household help, the rules are simpler and specific: the law governing kasambahays requires employers to keep copies of payslips for three years.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">SSS, PhilHealth, and Pag-IBIG Records</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">It is tempting to lump these in with the general three-year rule, but that is an oversimplification. Each agency has its own rules on contribution reporting, benefit claims, and verification, and those do not necessarily mirror the Labor Code's three-year figure. As a matter of practice, many employers keep these records for a longer internal period than three years, simply because an employee's contribution history can matter for benefit claims many years down the road — well past the point a stricter three-year policy would have allowed disposal.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Data Privacy Angle</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Data Privacy Act adds an overlay on top of all the rules above. Its principle is not "keep everything as long as possible just in case" — it is "keep it only as long as the law requires or as long as you actually need it for a legitimate purpose, then dispose of it securely." A payroll file might contain salary details, bank information, medical information, and ID copies all at once; not all of that needs the same retention period just because one piece of it happens to be tax-relevant. Where practical, separate sensitive personal data from the rest so it is not held longer than necessary.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Golden Rule: Legal Hold Beats Every Retention Schedule</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Whatever ordinary retention period applies, it should not be used as a basis for disposal when the record is relevant to an actual, pending, threatened, or reasonably foreseeable claim, audit, investigation, or government inspection. In that situation, the record should remain subject to a legal hold until the matter is resolved and the hold is formally lifted. This applies most often to termination records, disciplinary files, payroll and time records tied to a wage claim, and any workplace accident documentation connected to a compensation dispute.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Happens to Records When a Business Closes or Changes Hands?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">When a business closes, merges, or changes hands, records that remain legally required or are necessary to establish employment, wage, benefit, tax, or safety-related rights should not simply be discarded. They should be preserved and, where applicable, transferred to the successor entity or otherwise retained in accordance with the requirements of the closure or transfer process.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Putting It All Together</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If you are building (or cleaning up) your company's records retention policy, a good working structure looks like this:</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Employment records</strong> required to be maintained under labor rules — generally at least 3 years from the last entry. Individual 201-file documents may be subject to a different or longer period depending on their nature and other applicable laws.<br>
        <strong>Tax and accounting records</strong> — 10 years where covered by BIR retention rules, and longer where a pending tax matter requires continued preservation.<br>
        <strong>OSH accident/illness records</strong> — no fixed period; keep as long as reasonably necessary and available for inspection.<br>
        <strong>Kasambahay payslips</strong> — 3 years.<br>
        <strong>SSS/PhilHealth/Pag-IBIG records</strong> — per each agency's own rules; consider a longer internal period.<br>
        <strong>Anything under legal hold</strong> — kept until the matter is fully resolved, regardless of any other schedule.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Retention policies are not just about compliance — they are also about knowing when it is finally safe to let go. A little organization now saves a lot of scrambling later, whether that is during a DOLE inspection, a BIR audit, or an employee's benefit claim.</p>
