    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Were You Really Fired? The First Question Every Labor Case Must Answer</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on November 3, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Picture this. An employee has a heated exchange with a supervisor, and the next morning a message arrives: "Do not come in until we talk." There is no termination letter, no one said "you are fired," and nobody has explained what happens next. Is that a dismissal?</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The honest answer is: it depends. A short, temporary instruction may not be a dismissal. But if the employee is kept out indefinitely, with no clear reason or indication that work will resume, the question becomes whether the employer has effectively ended the employment.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">That is why Philippine labor law asks a basic question before anything else: was the employee actually dismissed? Let us look at how the courts answer it.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Why This Question Comes First</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Was there a valid reason? Was the employee given a chance to explain? Is the employee entitled to backwages? These questions matter, but they arise only after dismissal has been established.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Lawyers call this the "fact of dismissal." It simply means proof that the employer actually ended the employment relationship. If the employee cannot establish that fact, the employer generally does not have to prove the legality of a dismissal that has not been shown to exist.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Counts as a Dismissal?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 10px;">A dismissal does not always come in the form of a written notice. It may be shown by the employer's words or conduct, such as:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li>A written termination notice or memo</li>
            <li>A clear verbal order not to return to work</li>
            <li>Being refused entry to the workplace despite being ready and willing to work</li>
            <li>Being removed from the work schedule or otherwise prevented from performing one's job</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In <em>Amor v. Constant Packaging Corporation</em> (G.R. No. 259988, May 19, 2025), the workers were paid on pakyaw basis. When they reported for work, the security guard prevented them from entering the premises. The employer denied dismissing them, but the Supreme Court found that 12 workers had been illegally dismissed. They were ready and willing to work but had been arbitrarily prevented from doing so. A written termination letter was not necessary to establish what had happened.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Not every instruction to stay home, however, amounts to dismissal. It may be a temporary measure during an investigation or a legitimate work arrangement. The important question is whether the employer's words and actions show that the employment has actually ended.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Who Has to Prove It?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The employee who claims to have been dismissed must first establish that dismissal through substantial evidence. This can be difficult because employees may have limited access to company records. But that is where the legal inquiry begins.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Once dismissal is established, the burden shifts to the employer. The employer must prove that the dismissal was for a valid cause and that the required procedure was followed.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">There is an important distinction when the employer raises defenses such as resignation or abandonment. The employer must prove those defenses. It cannot simply deny dismissal and then rely on an unsupported claim that the employee voluntarily left.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Evidence Can Establish Dismissal?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The employee must establish dismissal through substantial evidence. The Supreme Court has emphasized that the proof must be clear, positive and convincing. A bare statement that "I was fired" is not enough. The employee must point to evidence of what the employer actually said or did.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 10px;">Useful evidence may include:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li>Text messages, emails, chat messages, memos, and written notices</li>
            <li>Work schedules, attendance records, and payslips showing when the employee stopped being allowed to work or stopped being paid</li>
            <li>Statements from co-workers who witnessed the incident</li>
            <li>Security logs, access-card records, or available CCTV footage</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A screenshot or message can be useful evidence when it is authentic and clearly establishes what was communicated. The surrounding circumstances also matter.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">When the Dismissal Was Only Spoken</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Verbal dismissals often become difficult cases. The employee says, "My supervisor told me never to return." The employer says, "We never dismissed anyone." Without a letter or message, the circumstances and the credibility of the evidence become especially important.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In <em>Reyes v. Global Beer Below Zero, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 222816, October 4, 2017), the Supreme Court explained that a verbal command not to report for work may constitute an overt act of dismissal when it comes from a person who has the authority to terminate the employee. The instruction came from the employee's immediate superior, who had such authority.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court applied the same principle in <em>Tapia v. GA2 Pharmaceutical, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 235725, September 28, 2022). The company's general manager ordered Tapia to go home and never come back after he refused to sign a resignation letter. Tapia promptly challenged what had happened. The Court found that the general manager's instruction, considered together with the surrounding circumstances, established the fact of dismissal.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">But verbal allegations do not automatically succeed. In <em>Pu-od v. Ablaze Builders, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 230791, November 20, 2017), the employees claimed that a project engineer had verbally dismissed them. They could not adequately identify the person or establish that the project engineer had authority to terminate their employment. The Court found their evidence insufficient.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The practical lesson is straightforward: identify who gave the instruction, explain what was said and when, establish the person's authority, and preserve any supporting messages or witness accounts. What happened immediately afterward may also help establish whether the instruction was genuinely a decision to end employment.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">"He Resigned" and "He Abandoned His Job"</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">When an employer denies dismissal, resignation and abandonment are common explanations. But both must be supported by evidence.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Resignation.</strong> A resignation must be voluntary and reflect the employee's genuine intention to leave. A signed letter does not settle the issue if the circumstances show that the employee was pressured or did not freely choose to relinquish the job.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In <em>Gabrillo v. Tri-J Marketing, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 279863, August 26, 2026), the Supreme Court found constructive dismissal after the employee was pressured to resign under threat of criminal prosecution following an inventory audit. The Court held that the circumstances showed that the resignation was not genuinely voluntary. It also found that the employer failed to establish its defenses. Because reinstatement was no longer feasible, the Court ordered separation pay in lieu of reinstatement, together with the other reliefs awarded in the case.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Abandonment.</strong> It is not enough that an employee stopped reporting for work. The employer must establish both the employee's failure to report without a valid reason and a clear intention to sever the employment relationship. The second element is often the more difficult one to prove.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In <em>Carpio v. Green Era Biotech Corp.</em> (G.R. No. 267217, November 19, 2025), the Supreme Court reiterated that absence alone does not establish abandonment. Carpio attempted to return to work but was no longer allowed to enter the premises. He then pursued an illegal dismissal complaint. The Court found that he had not abandoned his employment and that dismissal was too harsh a penalty for his unauthorized absences. It declared the dismissal illegal and ordered reinstatement, but without backwages, because the employer acted in good faith on the basis of his unauthorized absences. If reinstatement was no longer possible, separation pay was to be awarded in lieu of reinstatement.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What About Constructive Dismissal?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The same first question applies: has the employee established that the employment effectively ended? The difference is that constructive dismissal may happen without an express order to leave.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 10px;">Constructive dismissal occurs when continued employment becomes unreasonable or unlikely, or when the employer makes working conditions so difficult or prejudicial that a reasonable person would feel compelled to leave. Examples may include:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li>A demotion or substantial reduction in pay without a valid basis</li>
            <li>A transfer made in bad faith or without a legitimate business reason</li>
            <li>Persistent harassment or humiliating treatment</li>
            <li>Pressure to resign under circumstances that leave no real choice</li>
            <li>Being stripped of meaningful duties in a way that effectively pushes the employee out</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The employee must first establish the circumstances showing that the employer's conduct effectively forced the employee out. The employer may then explain and justify its actions, including by showing a legitimate business reason and good faith.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The distinction is illustrated by <em>Amor</em>. The Court found illegal dismissal for the workers who were prevented from entering the premises, but two other workers failed to establish the circumstances they claimed amounted to constructive dismissal.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A Word About Preventive Suspension</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">"Do not report for work" does not always mean "you are fired." An employer may impose preventive suspension during an investigation if the employee's continued presence poses a serious and imminent threat to the life or property of the employer or the employee's co-workers. This is governed by Book V, Rule XXIII, Sections 8 and 9 of the Omnibus Rules Implementing the Labor Code.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Preventive suspension is generally limited to 30 days. After that, the employer must reinstate the employee or extend the suspension while paying the required wages and benefits. It should not be used as an indefinite way of keeping an employee away from work.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An unexplained, indefinite exclusion from the workplace is precisely where the question arises: is this still a temporary measure, or has the employer effectively ended the employment? Employers should document the reason for preventive suspension, communicate its duration, and comply with the applicable rules.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Happens If the Dismissal Is Not Proven?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If the employee cannot establish that a dismissal took place, the illegal dismissal claim generally cannot succeed. The employer's burden to prove a valid cause and compliance with dismissal procedure arises only after the fact of dismissal has been established.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">But the employer cannot automatically treat the employee as having abandoned the job. Abandonment is a separate defense that the employer must prove. If neither dismissal nor abandonment is established, the employment relationship is generally treated as continuing.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Back to work, without backwages.</strong> As a general rule, the employee should be allowed to return to work without backwages for the period when no work was performed. This is not reinstatement as a remedy for illegal dismissal. It recognizes that the employment relationship was never severed. In <em>Rodriguez v. Sintron Systems, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 240254, July 24, 2019), the Court explained that the employer must accept the employee back. There is also no separation pay, since the employment was never terminated. If the employee voluntarily chooses not to return, the employee may be considered to have resigned. The Court applied the same general approach in <em>Samillano v. Valdez Security and Investigation Agency, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 239396, June 23, 2020).</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>When each party bears his or her own loss.</strong> There are cases where returning to work is no longer practicable. In <em>Pu-od v. Ablaze Builders, Inc.</em>, the employees no longer wanted to return, had found other employment, and reinstatement was no longer feasible. Since they failed to prove dismissal and the employer failed to prove abandonment, the Court ruled that each party must bear his or her own loss. Neither backwages nor separation pay was awarded.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This does not mean that strained relations alone justify keeping an employee out of work. In <em>Rodriguez</em>, the Court rejected that reasoning and recognized the employee's right to return. The circumstances of each case matter.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Finally, the failure to prove dismissal does not automatically extinguish independent money claims. Unpaid wages and benefits such as holiday pay, service incentive leave, and 13th month pay may still be considered if the employee establishes entitlement to them under the law. Each claim must be examined separately.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Practical Tips</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><strong>For employees:</strong></p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li>Save messages, emails, memos, and notices concerning your employment.</li>
            <li>If told verbally not to report for work, send a neutral message confirming what was said and asking whether you are still expected to report. Keep the response, if any.</li>
            <li>Do not sign a resignation letter unless you genuinely intend to resign.</li>
            <li>If you are turned away from work, document the incident and any attempt to clarify your employment status.</li>
            <li>Act promptly. Delays can make it harder to preserve evidence and establish what happened.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><strong>For employers:</strong></p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 20px; padding-left: 25px;">
            <li>Communicate employment decisions and their reasons clearly and in writing.</li>
            <li>If you impose preventive suspension, document the grounds and duration and observe the applicable 30-day rule.</li>
            <li>If you believe an employee has abandoned work, document reasonable attempts to contact the employee and direct the employee to explain or return.</li>
            <li>Do not use threats or pressure to obtain a resignation. Follow the proper disciplinary process.</li>
            <li>Remember that a sound reason for dismissal does not excuse failure to observe the required procedure.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Takeaway</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Three ideas matter. First, the fact of dismissal comes before the question of whether the dismissal was lawful. Second, the employee must first establish dismissal, while the employer must prove defenses such as resignation or abandonment. Third, evidence matters. A clear record of what was said, what was done, and what happened afterward can make all the difference.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">If you are facing a situation like the one at the beginning of this article, document what happened and preserve the records you have. Getting proper advice early can help clarify your position before the facts become harder to establish.</p>
