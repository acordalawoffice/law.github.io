    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Injured While Playing Basketball at Sea: What the Supreme Court Really Said in <em>Arriesgado</em></h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on September 23, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">What happens when a seafarer gets injured while playing basketball, not while standing watch, fixing machinery, or doing any assigned task on the ship?</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">At first glance, it might seem like a purely personal accident. Basketball is recreation, not work, right?</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">But for a seafarer, the line between "work" and "off-duty life" is not as clear-cut as it is for someone who can simply go home at the end of a shift.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">In the recent case of <strong><em>Ernesto D. Arriesgado v. Multinational Maritime, Inc., MMS Co., Ltd. and/or Capt. Banny B. Briones</em></strong> (G.R. No. 275424, April 29, 2026), the Supreme Court tackled exactly this kind of situation. The Court ruled that Arriesgado's basketball injury was work-related. But the decision did not stop there. It also laid down important reminders about a company's duties when it comes to medical treatment, the final medical assessment, giving the seafarer copies of his own medical records, sickness allowance, and what happens when a company falls short of its obligations under the standard seafarer employment contract.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Story, in Plain Terms</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Ernesto Arriesgado worked as an oiler and was deployed on board the <em>MV Kariyushi Leader</em> in October 2020. On January 10, 2021, he joined a basketball game between the vessel's deck and engine teams.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">During the game, he was hit in the left abdomen while pivoting suddenly. He felt severe pain and had trouble breathing, so he had to stop playing. He reported it to the duty officer, got some initial treatment, and continued doing light duties while being monitored.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">His condition did not improve. He was later examined in Japan, declared unfit for duty, and sent home to the Philippines on January 25, 2021.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Back home, he was placed under the care of the company-designated physician, but things eventually broke down. The doctor never issued a proper, final assessment within the time the law allows. A later report even questioned Arriesgado's attitude, suggesting he no longer wanted to go back to sea duty and was just after a payout.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Arriesgado then went to see his own doctor, who declared him permanently unfit for further sea service. The disagreement between the two sides eventually made its way all the way up to the Supreme Court.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Question One: Can a Basketball Injury Really Be "Work-Related"?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Yes, but not simply because it happened on a ship.</strong></p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Under the seafarer's standard contract, a work-related injury is one that "arises out of and in the course of employment." The Court pointed out that this does not require the seafarer to be doing his actual job duties at the exact moment he got hurt.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This matters a lot for seafarers, because their situation is different from land-based workers. By the nature of the job, they are required to stay on board the vessel for the whole length of their contract. They cannot just leave the premises after their shift the way an office worker can.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">To explain this, the Court leaned on two related legal ideas: the <strong>Bunkhouse Rule</strong> and the <strong>Personal Comfort Doctrine</strong>.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Bunkhouse Rule: When the Ship Is Also Your Home</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Bunkhouse Rule recognizes that when a job requires a worker to actually live on the employer's premises, things that happen while they are living there can still be connected to work, even outside regular working hours.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">For seafarers, this hits close to home, literally. They do not clock out and head home after a shift. The ship is their workplace and, for months at a time, also where they eat, sleep, and unwind.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Because of this, the Court recognized that a seafarer who gets hurt while resting, relaxing, or joining a recreational activity on board may still be considered within the course of his employment, especially when the activity is allowed (even just informally) by the company and helps the crew's well-being.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Personal Comfort Doctrine: Rest and Recreation Matter Too</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The <strong>Personal Comfort Doctrine</strong> says that ordinary personal needs, like eating, resting, or simply taking care of yourself, do not automatically take you outside the scope of your employment.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In a maritime setting, the Court extended this same reasoning to recreational activities that are allowed on board and meant to help keep the crew in good spirits.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This applied squarely to Arriesgado's situation. Tellingly, the company itself admitted that the basketball game was meant as recreation for the crew. The Court noted that a crew that is in better shape, mentally and physically, ultimately benefits the company too, through better performance on the job.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">That said, this does not mean every accident anywhere on a ship is automatically compensable. The Court was clear that there are still limits under the seafarer's standard contract.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What If the Seafarer Was the One at Fault?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The seafarer's standard contract does carve out an exception: no benefits are owed if the injury, disability, or death was caused by the seafarer's own willful act, a crime he committed, or an intentional violation of his duties, and the company is able to prove that connection.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court explained that it is the company's job to prove, with real evidence, that: (1) the injury is directly attributable to the seafarer, (2) the seafarer committed a crime or willfully broke his duties, and (3) there is a clear link between that act and the injury.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In Arriesgado's case, the company could not show that he did anything like that. Simply getting hurt through the ordinary bad luck of a basketball game was not enough, on its own, to defeat his claim.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Why the Court Brought Up Two Earlier Cases</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This case makes more sense when you compare it with two earlier Supreme Court rulings.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In an earlier case called <em>Arguilles</em>, the Court dealt with a seafarer who was also injured playing basketball during his free time on board. The Court used the same Bunkhouse Rule and Personal Comfort Doctrine reasoning and ruled that injury was work-related too.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The company in Arriesgado's case tried to argue that <em>Arguilles</em> should not apply here, because the union contract in that earlier case specifically treated recreational activities as part of the seafarer's onboard accommodation, while the contract covering Arriesgado did not have that same wording.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court did not buy that distinction. It explained that the seafarer's standard contract itself, together with the facts of the case and past rulings, was already enough basis to treat the injury as work-related, regardless of how the union contract was worded.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court also set this case apart from another earlier ruling called <em>Guerrero</em>, which involved a casino dealer injured while working out in the ship's gym on his own. The Court pointed out that <em>Guerrero</em> had some very different circumstances: the seafarer there gave conflicting stories about how he got hurt, only claimed a permanent disability late in the case, and his own doctor's opinion was not backed up by proper tests.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The takeaway here is important: <strong>the Supreme Court did not create a blanket rule that any recreational activity on a ship automatically counts as work-related.</strong> Each case still depends on the seafarer's actual contract, the specific facts, and existing case law.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Second, and Bigger, Issue: What Went Wrong With the Medical Assessment</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This is where the <em>Arriesgado</em> case becomes especially useful for seafarers pursuing disability claims of their own.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A company-designated physician does not get unlimited time to keep a seafarer under treatment without ever giving a final answer.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Here is the rule the Court reiterated:</p>

        <ol style="margin-bottom: 15px;">
            <li>The company doctor must issue a <strong>final, complete, and clear medical assessment</strong> within <strong>120 days</strong> from the seafarer's repatriation.</li>
            <li>If there is a valid reason for more treatment, this period can be extended to <strong>240 days</strong>.</li>
            <li>It is the company's job to prove that the extension was actually justified.</li>
            <li>If no valid final assessment is given within these periods, the seafarer's disability is automatically considered <strong>total and permanent</strong> by law.</li>
        </ol>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court also clarified something that used to cause confusion: even though older cases sometimes said the countdown starts from when the seafarer first reports to the company doctor, the correct starting point is actually the date the seafarer is repatriated.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">In Arriesgado's Case, the Clock Had Already Run Out</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Arriesgado was sent home on <strong>January 25, 2021</strong>. That means his 120-day countdown ended on <strong>May 25, 2021</strong>.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">But the company doctor never issued a valid, final, and clear assessment within that window.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A later report dated August 12, 2021 did not fix the problem either. The Court found that this report came way too late, and even then, it did not clearly explain the real extent of Arriesgado's injury or whether he could go back to work. Instead, it mostly just suggested he was faking his condition to get a payout.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court's message was simple: if the company genuinely believed Arriesgado was already fit to work, was not cooperating, or was not entitled to more treatment, the proper move was to say so clearly, through a timely and definite medical assessment, within the period the law allows. Silence and vague suspicion do not count.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What About the Three-Day Reporting Rule?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Seafarers are generally expected to report to the company within three days of being repatriated, for their post-employment medical check-up.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Arriesgado did not strictly meet that deadline. He was only examined by the company doctor on February 1, 2021, seven days after coming home.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">But that delay did not sink his case.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court found that he had substantially complied with the rule. He reached out to the ship's representative right after repatriation to ask for his medical check-up, but was told to first finish the mandatory COVID-19 quarantine before seeing the company doctor.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The Court made clear that the three-day rule is not a strict "miss it and you lose everything" kind of deadline. The reasons behind any delay still matter.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A Seafarer Has the Right to Know What the Company Doctor Found</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Another key part of the ruling has to do with medical records.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Under the seafarer's standard contract, when a seafarer asks for it, the company must give him copies of all his relevant medical reports and records, free of charge.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In Arriesgado's case, his medical reports were addressed only to the company and its representatives, almost as if they were confidential company documents. Despite his requests, he was never given copies of his own records or a proper final assessment.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court took this seriously. A seafarer cannot reasonably question or challenge a medical finding that was never even properly explained or given to him in the first place.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Because of this failure to hand over his medical records, on top of the company's delay in paying his sickness allowance, the Court awarded Arriesgado <strong>PHP 50,000 as a form of penalty (called nominal damages)</strong> for disregarding his rights.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Sickness Allowance Is a Separate Benefit From Disability Pay</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court also made clear that a company's duties to an injured seafarer do not begin and end with disability benefits alone.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Under the standard contract, a seafarer is also entitled to sickness allowance, basically his regular basic wage, counted from the day he signs off the ship until he is declared fit to work or his disability is assessed, for up to 120 days.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Arriesgado's basic monthly wage was <strong>USD 641.00</strong>, so his full 120-day sickness allowance came out to <strong>USD 2,564.00</strong>.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Since the company had already paid him <strong>PHP 91,575.65</strong> as sickness allowance, that amount was deducted from what was still owed to him. The Court also called out the company for paying the second installment very late, reminding everyone that sickness allowance is supposed to be paid regularly, at least once a month, not whenever convenient.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">So What Did the Supreme Court Finally Award?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court sided with Arriesgado, reversing the Court of Appeals and bringing back the earlier NLRC ruling, with some adjustments.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The company was ordered to pay him, together with its officers:</p>

        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px;">
            <li><strong>USD 60,000.00</strong> as total and permanent disability benefits;</li>
            <li><strong>USD 2,564.00</strong>, minus the <strong>PHP 91,575.65</strong> already paid, as the remaining sickness allowance owed; and</li>
            <li><strong>PHP 50,000.00</strong> as a penalty for disregarding his rights.</li>
        </ul>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The dollar amounts are to be converted and paid in Philippine pesos based on the exchange rate at the time of payment, plus 6% interest per year from when the decision becomes final until it's fully paid.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Interestingly, though, the Supreme Court removed the earlier award of attorney's fees, finding no sufficient basis for it.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What This Case Means for Filipino Seafarers</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">There are a few practical lessons here.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>First, do not assume an injury does not count just because it happened during your free time.</strong> Since seafarers are required to live on board, injuries during onboard recreation can still be considered work-related, depending on the circumstances.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Second, report the injury right away.</strong> Tell your officer, get it documented, and comply with the post-employment medical check-up requirements once you are repatriated.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Third, keep cooperating with the company doctor.</strong> This case does not give seafarers a free pass to skip appointments or ignore medical advice. Failing to cooperate can genuinely hurt your claim.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Fourth, ask for your medical records.</strong> You are entitled to copies of your own medical reports, free of charge, any time you ask.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Fifth, keep track of the 120 and 240-day periods.</strong> These are not just technical deadlines. Missing them can mean your disability is automatically treated as total and permanent under the law.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Finally, remember that the details really do matter.</strong> This case should not be read as saying every shipboard injury is automatically compensable. The Court looked closely at the nature of seafarer employment, the facts of the basketball game, the seafarer's contract, the lack of any proof of wrongdoing on his part, his medical history, and the company's failure to properly assess and inform him.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Bigger Lesson: At Sea, "Off Duty" Does not Always Mean "Outside Work"</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Maybe the biggest takeaway from this case is that a seafarer's job cannot always be judged the same way as an ordinary land-based job.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A seafarer might finish his shift, change clothes, eat, rest, exercise, or join a game, but he is still on the ship, because his job requires him to live there.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">That does not mean the company has to answer for absolutely everything that happens on board. But it does mean the law has to take into account what life at sea actually looks like when deciding whether an injury is connected to work.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">That is exactly what the Supreme Court did in this case.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The basketball game was just for fun. The injury was an accident. But given everything that happened afterward, especially the company's failure to give a proper, timely medical assessment, the injury was still treated as work-related, and it made all the difference in the outcome of the case.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Need Help With a Seafarer Disability Claim?</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Seafarer disability cases often come down to small details that might seem unimportant at first: when the injury happened, when it was reported, when the seafarer was sent home, when (or if) the company doctor gave a final assessment, and whether the seafarer actually received his own medical records.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If you or someone in your family is dealing with a shipboard injury or a disputed disability claim, these small details can matter a lot. Do not assume your claim is a sure win or a lost cause without checking with a lawyer first.</p>
