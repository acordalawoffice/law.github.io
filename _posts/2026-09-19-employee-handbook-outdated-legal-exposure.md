    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Your Employee Handbook Is Five Years Old. Is It Still Doing Its Job?</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on September 19, 2026 &bull; Updated on November 21, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A company terminates an employee for breaking a workplace rule, such as repeated tardiness or unauthorized use of company property. The paperwork looks solid: a notice to explain, a hearing, and a notice of decision. Then, at the labor arbiter's office, the employee's lawyer asks a simple question: where is the rule that was supposedly broken, and can the company show that it was made known to this employee?</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The company produces a handbook, but it was last revised years ago, nobody can find a signed acknowledgment, and part of the disciplinary table no longer matches what the company actually enforces. The company may still win the case, since the outcome depends on the facts and the evidence. But it now has to work much harder to prove its side.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A Handbook Is Evidence, Not a Magic Shield</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Many business owners treat the handbook as a welcome packet that HR hands out on the first day and nobody reads again. In a dispute, it can play a much bigger role. When a company disciplines or dismisses an employee, it must show a valid reason and a proper process. The handbook is one of the ways a company can show what its rules are and that employees knew about them.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">It is important to be clear about its limits, though. A handbook is one piece of evidence among several. Whether a dismissal stands depends on the legal ground invoked, the proof of what the employee actually did, and whether the proper procedure was followed. A good handbook helps the company's case but does not win it, and a weak handbook hurts but does not automatically lose it. Still, written rules matter in specific ways. Under the DOLE rules on termination (<a href="https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/10/71535" target="_blank" rel="noopener" style="color: #2b6cb0; text-decoration: underline;">Department Order No. 147-15</a>), willful disobedience requires an order that was reasonable, lawful, and made known to the employee, and an act is treated as an analogous cause only if the company's rules or policies expressly specify it.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What the Notice to Explain Really Needs</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Before dismissing an employee for a just cause, an employer must observe what is commonly called the twin-notice rule: a first written notice that tells the employee what is being charged, a real chance to respond and be heard, and a second written notice stating the decision. The Supreme Court spelled this out in <a href="https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2007/jun2007/gr_166208_2007.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener" style="color: #2b6cb0; text-decoration: underline;"><em>King of Kings Transport, Inc. v. Mamac</em></a>. According to that decision, the first notice should give a detailed account of the facts and circumstances, and it should identify the company rule that was violated, if there is one, and/or the ground under Article 297 of the Labor Code that is being charged. A vague, general description of the charge is not enough. The notice should also give the employee a reasonable period to respond, which the Court construed as at least five calendar days from receipt, so that the employee can study the accusation, consult a lawyer or union officer, and gather evidence. The DOLE rules adopt the same standards.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In plain terms, the notice must tell the employee what they allegedly did, when and how, and which rule or legal ground applies, and then leave enough time to answer. A written company rule makes this much easier to do, and much easier to prove later. <em>King of Kings</em> speaks of the company rule, if any, and/or the Labor Code ground being charged, so a handbook entry is helpful but is not described as the only possible basis. A company that relies on a workplace rule should still be ready to show that the rule was reasonable and made known to employees.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Being heard does not always mean a formal, trial-type hearing. <em>King of Kings</em> called for a hearing or conference, but the Court later clarified, in <em>Perez v. Philippine Telegraph and Telephone Co.</em> (2009), that what the law requires is an ample opportunity to be heard, meaning any meaningful chance, verbal or written, to answer the charges and submit evidence. The 2017 decision in <a href="https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2017/jan2017/gr_207838_2017.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener" style="color: #2b6cb0; text-decoration: underline;"><em>Maula v. Ximex Delivery Express, Inc.</em></a> restates this rule, and the DOLE rules follow it. A formal hearing or conference becomes mandatory only when the employee asks for one in writing, when there are substantial disputes over the evidence, when a company rule or practice requires one, or when similar circumstances justify it. Since a handbook may itself promise a hearing, this is another reason to review what yours says.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Makes a Handbook Weak</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A handbook does not have to be long to be useful, but it should hold up when someone examines it closely. Common weak spots include the following:</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Vague, catch-all rules.</strong> A provision like "any act prejudicial to the company" with no further explanation gives management wide discretion and gives employees very little notice of what is actually prohibited.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Penalties that do not fit the offense.</strong> Discipline is expected to be proportionate to the seriousness of the violation. The Supreme Court has said that not every case of insubordination deserves dismissal, because the penalty must be commensurate with the gravity of the offense (<em>Maula</em>), so a table of penalties that treats minor and serious lapses alike invites challenge.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>No record that employees received it.</strong> A signed acknowledgment is a helpful way to show the handbook was given to and accessible to the employee. It proves receipt, though, and not that every rule is enforceable or that the company applied it fairly.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Rules on paper that nobody follows.</strong> A company that rarely enforces a rule, or enforces it only against some employees, will find it hard to convince anyone that the rule was taken seriously.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What a Handbook Should Address Today</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Work has changed in the last five years, and handbooks should reflect that. It helps to separate what the law expects from what is simply good practice.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Where the law sets expectations: harassment in the workplace.</strong> Under Section 17 of the <a href="https://issuances-library.senate.gov.ph/sites/default/files/2021-08/20190417-RA-11313-RRD.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener" style="color: #2b6cb0; text-decoration: underline;">Safe Spaces Act (Republic Act No. 11313)</a>, employers have a duty to prevent, deter, and punish gender-based sexual harassment in the workplace. This includes sharing the law with everyone in the workplace, providing preventive measures such as anti-sexual harassment seminars, creating a Committee on Decorum and Investigation (CODI), and developing, in consultation with the people in the workplace, a code of conduct or workplace policy. That policy must prohibit harassment, describe how the committee works, and set administrative penalties. The <a href="https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/2/96805" target="_blank" rel="noopener" style="color: #2b6cb0; text-decoration: underline;">Implementing Rules</a> supply a checklist for the committee. It should include at least one representative each from management, the supervisors, the rank-and-file employees, and the union, if there is one. It should be headed by a woman, with at least half of its members women. Its members should be impartial, not connected or related to the alleged perpetrator within the fourth degree of consanguinity or affinity, and without a prior record as a respondent in a sexual harassment case. The Rules also call for existing policies and committees created under the older Anti-Sexual Harassment Act (Republic Act No. 7877) to be amended to conform, so a handbook that still describes only the older framework deserves a second look. Because the Rules treat a workplace as any place where work is being done, inside or outside the usual premises, and the Act covers harassment through text messages, email, and other technology, the policy should also reach remote and hybrid settings.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Two further points are worth knowing. First, an anonymous report of harassment is sufficient notice to the employer, who must then verify it and refer it to the CODI. It does not count as a formal complaint, though, unless the victim makes it in his or her own name, and the CODI's ten-working-day period to investigate and decide, which does not include any appeal period, applies to written complaints. Second, employers may be held responsible for failing to implement their Section 17 duties, which carries a fine of five thousand to ten thousand pesos upon conviction, and for failing to act on reported harassment, which carries a fine of ten thousand to fifteen thousand pesos upon conviction. The code of conduct also gives the company a written standard for discipline, although any dismissal that follows must still go through the twin-notice process.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Where the law sets expectations: personal data.</strong> The <a href="https://www.privacy.gov.ph/data-privacy-act/" target="_blank" rel="noopener" style="color: #2b6cb0; text-decoration: underline;">Data Privacy Act (Republic Act No. 10173)</a> and its <a href="https://privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/IRR_RA-10173-as-amended.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener" style="color: #2b6cb0; text-decoration: underline;">Implementing Rules</a> cover private employers. Among other things, they ask employers to follow the principles of transparency, legitimate purpose, and proportionality; to keep documented data protection policies that are regularly reviewed and updated; to designate a person accountable for compliance; to train the employees who handle personal data; and to keep that data confidential even after employment ends. What is expected scales with the size of the business and the sensitivity of the data, and details such as health, age, and marital status carry stricter conditions. The Rules also require notifying the National Privacy Commission and the affected persons within seventy-two hours, counted from the employer's knowledge of, or reasonable belief that, a breach requiring notification has occurred. The law relaxes the advance-notice rule for processing that is necessary or desirable in an employment relationship, but employees should still be able to understand, in plain language, what information is collected and why. A handbook section or a separate privacy notice is a practical way to do this, although the law does not require the handbook itself to carry it.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Where the choice is the employer's.</strong> Rules on remote or hybrid arrangements, attendance monitoring, use of company equipment, and social media conduct are not each required by a specific law to appear in a handbook. If these issues matter to the business, written and reasonable rules give both sides something clear to rely on, and an employer without them must be ready to show that the standard it applied was reasonable and properly communicated.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Practical Takeaway</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The handbook is not a contract in the ordinary sense, but it is a written record of how the company says it runs its workplace. Labor tribunals may look at it, together with the company's actual practices, to judge whether a rule was reasonable, known to employees, and fairly applied. Its legal effect depends on how it is worded and on the circumstances of each case.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">A sensible habit is to review the handbook together with the rest of the paper trail: the acknowledgment records, the disciplinary files, the harassment policy and committee, the privacy and security policies, and the way rules are actually enforced day to day. If your handbook was written before hybrid work became common, it is a good time to take a fresh look at all of them.</p>
