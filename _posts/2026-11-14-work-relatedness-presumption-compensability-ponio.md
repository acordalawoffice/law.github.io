    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">When Work-Relatedness Is Presumed, Is Compensability Also Presumed?</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on November 14, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px; font-size: 0.92rem; color: #718096; text-align: left;"><strong>Case:</strong> <em>RCCL Crew Management, Inc. and Royal Caribbean Cruise, Ltd. v. Ponio</em><br><strong>G.R. No. 263434 | July 1, 2026 | En Banc</strong></p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Imagine a seafarer who falls ill while working aboard a ship. The illness is not one of the occupational diseases listed in the 2010 POEA Standard Employment Contract (POEA-SEC). What happens to the claim for disability benefits?</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The contract gives the seafarer a helpful starting point: when an illness not covered by the occupational-disease provisions appears or occurs during the term of employment, it is disputably presumed to be work-related, unless the employer rebuts that presumption. But that raises a question that the Supreme Court itself answered in different ways over the years. Does this presumption stop at work-relatedness, so that the seafarer must still prove every requirement for compensation? Or does it also cover compensability, so that the employer must come forward with evidence if it wishes to deny the claim?</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">In its July 1, 2026 En Banc decision in <em>RCCL Crew Management, Inc. v. Ponio</em>, the Supreme Court settled the matter. The presumption of work-relatedness under Section 20(A)(4) of the 2010 POEA-SEC automatically carries with it a presumption of compensability, which the employer may still rebut with evidence.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The story behind the case</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Jonathan Ian H. Ponio had been repeatedly hired by RCCL Crew Management, Inc. for its foreign principal, Royal Caribbean Cruise, Ltd. On March 20, 2017, he took his pre-employment medical examination and was declared fit for duty without restrictions. His records showed no hearing loss, and both of his ears were rated adequate in the hearing test.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">He signed a nine-month contract as a galley steward and boarded the <em>Anthem of the Seas</em> on April 14, 2017. His work included cleaning and sanitizing the galley, washing kitchen equipment and garbage bins, segregating and removing waste, and moving supplies around.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In August 2017, about four months into his contract, he began losing hearing in his right ear. He told the ship doctor, who gave him ear drops and antibiotics. He was later sent for a shoreside check-up and was medically repatriated on October 30, 2017. The company-designated physician diagnosed chronic otitis media (an ear infection) with a possible growth called cholesteatoma. Ponio underwent surgery and then six months of anti-tuberculosis treatment.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">On June 13, 2018, the company-designated physician declared his final diagnosis to be tuberculous otitis media in the right ear and gave him a Grade 11 disability rating for the loss of hearing in one ear. Ponio consulted his own doctor, who concluded that he would have permanent, severe hearing loss in that ear and was permanently unfit for sea duty.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The dispute went to voluntary arbitration, where Ponio was awarded USD 95,000.00 (the amount under the parties&rsquo; collective bargaining agreement, or CBA) plus ten percent attorney&rsquo;s fees. The Court of Appeals affirmed and added six percent annual legal interest. The employers then went to the Supreme Court, insisting among other things that Ponio should have proven, on his own, that his illness was work-related.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Two provisions, one long-running debate</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Section 20(A)(4)</strong> says that illnesses not listed in Section 32-A are disputably presumed to be work-related. &ldquo;Disputably&rdquo; simply means that the employer may present evidence to prove the opposite. The presumption applies when the illness manifests or occurs during the term of employment. (The provision itself speaks of illnesses not listed in Section 32, and the Court reads it together with the occupational-disease list in Section 32-A.)</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Section 32-A</strong> lists the occupational diseases and sets four conditions for compensation: the seafarer&rsquo;s work involved the described risks; the disease was contracted because of exposure to those risks; it was contracted within the period of exposure and under the factors needed to contract it; and there was no notorious negligence on the seafarer&rsquo;s part.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The puzzle was this: if an illness is not even on the list, must the seafarer still prove those four conditions one by one? As <em>Ponio</em> shows, an illness may even appear in the list and still fall outside its coverage when the specific working conditions required by the listing are absent.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Three approaches in earlier decisions</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court acknowledged that its earlier decisions followed three different paths.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>First approach:</strong> The seafarer must still prove with substantial evidence that the working conditions caused the illness or at least increased the risk of getting it. Cases such as <em>Casomo v. Career Philippines Shipmanagement, Inc.</em> (2012), <em>Romana v. Magsaysay Maritime Corp.</em> (2017), <em>Albarracin v. Philippine Transworld Shipping</em> (2018), <em>Jebsens Maritime, Inc. v. Alcibar</em> (2019), <em>Destriza v. Fair Shipping Corp.</em> (2021), and <em>Amores, Jr. v. Goldroute Maritime, Inc.</em> (2024) took this view.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Second approach:</strong> The illness is presumed work-related, but the seafarer must still prove the Section 32-A requirements, including the absence of notorious negligence. <em>Atienza v. Orophil Shipping International Co., Inc.</em> (2017) and <em>Ampolitod v. Top Ever Marine Management Phils., Inc.</em> (2024) are examples.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Third approach:</strong> The presumption of work-relatedness automatically brings along a presumption of compensability. <em>Hernandez v. Sealion Maritime Services, Corp.</em> (2021) took this view.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">These were not just different ways of saying the same thing. Each one placed the burden of proof on a different party.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Court chooses the third approach</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court did not rest on the presumption alone. It looked at the history of compensation laws, the employer&rsquo;s role in evaluating seafarer claims, and the practical realities of proving what happens at work at sea.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>The history of compensation law.</strong> Under the old Workmen&rsquo;s Compensation Act, once an employee showed that an illness supervened in the course of employment, the law presumed that it arose out of and in the course of that employment, or was aggravated by it. The employer could overcome the presumption with evidence to the contrary. Presidential Decree No. 626 later dropped that presumption. As the Court explained, claims were then decided by a neutral government body, the Employees&rsquo; Compensation Commission, with no employer opposing the claim. The Court observed that this reason does not exist for seafarers. Their employers do not contribute to the State Insurance Fund, and it is the employer, through the company-designated physician, who first decides whether the illness is work-related and compensable.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>The employer knows the work.</strong> The first three conditions in Section 32-A concern the seafarer&rsquo;s duties, working conditions, and exposure. These are matters the employer knows best. It has the personnel files and understands the physical demands of the job.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>The pre-employment medical examination.</strong> A pre-employment medical examination is not an exhaustive test that can detect every possible illness. Still, it is useful evidence of the seafarer&rsquo;s health before deployment. When the seafarer is declared fit for work and the illness manifests only during the contract, it is reasonable to presume, in the absence of contrary evidence, that the illness supervened during employment.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;"><strong>Protection of workers.</strong> Compensation laws are social legislation and must be read generously in favor of workers, all the more for Filipino seafarers who work far from home. The benefits under the POEA-SEC should also approximate, or even exceed, what employees receive under the Labor Code.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What about notorious negligence?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">One concern was that Section 32-A requires an absence of notorious negligence on the seafarer&rsquo;s part, and a presumption of work-relatedness should not automatically prove that.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court answered by reading Section 32-A together with Section 20(D), which denies benefits for injuries resulting from the seafarer&rsquo;s willful or criminal act or intentional breach of duty, provided the employer can prove it. Notorious negligence is far more than ordinary carelessness. It involves deliberately disregarding one&rsquo;s own safety or consciously refusing to do one&rsquo;s duty. Since the employer is the one invoking it to defeat the claim, the employer must prove it. The law presumes that a person takes ordinary care of his or her concerns. Thus, negligence, particularly the deliberate conduct contemplated by notorious negligence, must be established by the party asserting it.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">In short, the Court did not ignore the fourth condition. It explained why proving it falls on the employer.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">How the burden moves</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Court laid out a clear sequence. First, the seafarer shows with substantial evidence that the illness, which is not covered by Section 32-A, manifested or occurred during the term of employment. The presumptions of work-relatedness and compensability then arise. Second, the employer must prove either that the illness is not compensable because the Section 32-A requirements are not met (for example, the illness was already there before boarding, or the work did not expose the seafarer to the risk), or that it has an affirmative defense, such as notorious negligence, a willful or criminal act or intentional breach of duty, or the seafarer&rsquo;s knowing concealment of a pre-existing illness or condition during the pre-employment examination.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Third, if the employer does present proof that the illness is not work-related, the burden shifts back to the seafarer to prove otherwise. The presumption is therefore strong, but it is not absolute.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Ponio&rsquo;s own illness</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Here is an interesting detail. Otitis media actually appears in the Section 32-A list. However, the list covers it only when the seafarer&rsquo;s work involves animals infected with anthrax or the handling of animal carcasses and similar materials. Ponio did not allege or prove this kind of work, so the listing could not be applied to him, and the Court turned to Section 20(A)(4). Because the symptoms appeared during his contract, the presumption arose, and the employers, who offered no evidence to rebut it, could not overcome it.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The Court added that Ponio would have succeeded even without the presumption. He was declared fit before boarding with normal hearing, and the illness appeared only afterward. The medical records described an infection, treated with antibiotics and referred to an infectious disease specialist. His duties in the galley included cleaning garbage bins and removing waste, and the Court found it reasonable to conclude that such waste can carry germs that cause ear infections.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Why Ponio Was Considered Totally and Permanently Disabled</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Interestingly, the award did not rest on the presumption alone. The Court accepted that the company-designated physician needed the extended 240-day period, because Ponio required six months of treatment. The final report and the Grade 11 rating were issued on June 13, 2018, within that period.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The problem was that the record contained no proof that Ponio was given a copy of these final findings. The only evidence was a letter from his lawyer saying that the company doctor told him of the Grade 11 rating but gave him no copy of the assessment. The Court repeated that a verbal notice is not enough. The seafarer must be personally given a written copy of the final medical assessment, or it must be sent by other means allowed by present rules of procedure, so that he can decide whether to accept it or consult another doctor. Without it, the seafarer is deemed totally and permanently disabled by operation of law.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">As for the amount, the CBA covered only illnesses on the International Labour Organization list and pointed to the POEA-SEC for other illnesses. Still, because the CBA benefit of USD 95,000.00 is higher than the POEA-SEC amount for a total and permanent disability (USD 60,000.00), and the CBA expressly includes amounts payable under POEA rules, the higher amount was awarded. The Court also granted attorney&rsquo;s fees of ten percent, since Ponio was forced to litigate to protect his rights, and six percent annual legal interest from the finality of its decision until full payment.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The claims process still matters</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The ruling does not make every illness at sea automatically compensable. A medically repatriated seafarer must still report to the company-designated physician within three working days from arrival (or give written notice to the agency within that period if physically unable), and must keep to the required follow-up check-ups. Failing to comply with mandatory reporting can cost the seafarer the right to these benefits.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The Court also clarified that this process, including the three-day rule and the conflict-resolution mechanism that allows referral to a third doctor, applies to disputes involving work-relatedness as well. It rejected the reading in earlier cases (<em>Balbarino</em> and <em>Leonis</em>) that limited this process to questions of fitness and disability grade. The separate concurring opinions of Justices Leonen and Caguioa add further discussion of the policy reasons behind the ruling, the allocation of the evidentiary burden, and the role of the third-doctor mechanism.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What this means for employers and seafarers</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>For manning agencies and shipowners:</strong> A denial can no longer rest on the argument that the illness is not listed or that the seafarer did not prove each requirement. Employers should make sure the company-designated physician has a complete picture of the seafarer&rsquo;s duties, work environment, exposure, medical history, and the timing of the illness. If the employer believes the illness is not work-related, or that a defense applies, it must be ready to prove it. Employers should also make sure that the seafarer receives a written copy of the final assessment within the required periods.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;"><strong>For seafarers:</strong> Report your symptoms while on board, ask for medical attention, keep copies of every medical record and report, and comply with the post-repatriation examination and follow-up schedule. Always ask for a written copy of the final medical assessment. The presumption is a real protection, but it works best when the paper trail is complete.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A clearer rule, but not an automatic award</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The importance of <em>Ponio</em> goes beyond one seafarer&rsquo;s award. The En Banc ended a long-standing difference in the Court&rsquo;s decisions. When an unlisted illness appears during a seafarer&rsquo;s contract, the law presumes that it is work-related and compensable. The employer may rebut that presumption, but only with evidence.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">In plain terms: the presumption is not an automatic award. It is a rule about who must bring evidence first, and a claim cannot be turned away by handing the entire burden back to the seafarer. And for seafarers, the protection written into the POEA-SEC now has real weight in the courtroom.</p>
