    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Just Cause vs. Authorized Cause: Why the Difference Decides How Much You Owe</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on September 19, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Two employees from the same company lose their jobs in the same week. One walks away with a separation pay check. The other does not get a single peso beyond his last salary. Both were told the same thing: "You are dismissed." So why the different outcomes?</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The answer lies in a distinction that a lot of employers — and even more employees — never quite learn until it costs them. Philippine labor law splits every valid dismissal into two very different categories, and mixing them up is one of the most expensive mistakes a business can make.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Two Reasons to Let Someone Go, Two Different Stories</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Strip away the legal terms and the idea is actually simple. There are only two honest reasons to end someone's employment: either the employee did something wrong, or the business genuinely needs to let them go through no fault of their own.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The first is called <strong>just cause</strong>. It is about fault — something the employee did or failed to do. The second is called <strong>authorized cause</strong>. It is about circumstance — the business closing, downsizing, or facing a situation that has nothing to do with the employee's conduct or performance.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">That one distinction — fault versus circumstance — is what the rest of the law hangs on: what the employer owes, what paperwork is required, and how much risk is on the table if something is done wrong.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Just Cause: When the Employee Is the Reason</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Just cause covers situations where the employee's own actions broke the trust the job depends on. The law lists the grounds: serious misconduct, willful disobedience of a lawful order, gross and habitual neglect of duties, fraud or willful breach of the trust the employer placed in the employee, a crime or offense committed against the employer, the employer's family, or the employer's representatives, and other causes similar to these.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Think of things like theft, falsifying company records, repeated and deliberate defiance of reasonable instructions, or being seriously and consistently careless in a way that puts the business at risk. These are not minor slip-ups — the law reserves just cause for offenses serious enough to justify losing one's job entirely. And that last item on the list, "other similar causes," is not a blank check: under DOLE's rules, an offense only counts if the company's written rules or policies already spell it out.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Here is the part that surprises a lot of people: when a dismissal is for just cause, the law does not require the employer to pay separation pay. The reasoning is straightforward — the employee's own wrongdoing caused the termination, so there is no legal obligation to soften the landing. Some employers still give something out of goodwill, and a company policy or a union agreement (CBA) may promise it, but the law itself does not demand it.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Occasionally, labor tribunals have awarded a modest "financial assistance" anyway, out of compassion. But the Supreme Court has narrowed this a great deal. It is off the table for dishonesty, serious misconduct, willful disobedience, gross and habitual neglect, breach of trust, or a crime against the employer. It may still come up for performance-type reasons such as inefficiency, depending on things like length of service and whether it was a first offense. So neither side should count on it.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Authorized Cause: When the Business Is the Reason</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Authorized cause dismissals are a completely different animal. Here, the employee did nothing wrong. The law recognizes only a handful of situations:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 6px;"><strong>Labor-saving devices</strong> — machines or systems that make some jobs unnecessary.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 6px;"><strong>Redundancy</strong> — a position that is more than the business actually needs.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 6px;"><strong>Retrenchment</strong> — cutting staff to prevent business losses.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 6px;"><strong>Closure</strong> — the business shutting down, fully or partly.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 6px;"><strong>Serious illness</strong> — under strict conditions, explained below.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Because the employee is not at fault, the law requires the employer to soften the blow financially. Separation pay is mandatory in almost every case, and the amount depends on the ground:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 6px;"><strong>Labor-saving devices or redundancy:</strong> at least one month's pay, or one month's pay for every year of service, whichever is higher.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 6px;"><strong>Retrenchment, closure not caused by serious losses, or illness:</strong> at least one month's pay, or half a month's pay for every year of service, whichever is higher.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 6px;"><strong>Closure caused by serious business losses:</strong> no separation pay is required.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">When counting years of service, a leftover stretch of at least six months counts as a full year. Getting this computation wrong is a common source of disputes.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Naming an Authorized Cause Is not Enough</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">It is not enough for an employer to say "redundancy" or "retrenchment." The reason has to be real, provable, and carried out in good faith. A redundancy needs solid evidence that the position is truly excess, such as a new staffing pattern or a management-approved restructuring plan. Retrenchment needs proof that the losses are substantial and either already happening or clearly about to happen.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">For labor-saving devices, redundancy, and retrenchment, the choice of who goes must also follow fair and reasonable criteria, such as seniority, efficiency, and status. It cannot be based on who the boss happens to like less. A reorganization used as a cover to remove an unwanted employee is exactly the kind of thing that gets dismissals struck down.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Illness is the strictest of all. It is not enough that an employee is sick, or that the sickness is inconvenient for the company. Continued work must be prohibited by law or harmful to the health of the employee or the co-workers, and a competent public health authority must certify that the illness cannot be cured within six months even with proper treatment. The employer's own opinion does not count. Without that certification, the dismissal will not stand, however sincere the employer's concern may be.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Where Employers Get Burned</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The costliest mistakes happen when the two categories get blurred — usually on purpose, sometimes out of ignorance. A business quietly struggling might label a termination as a performance or misconduct issue (just cause) when what is actually happening is a redundancy (authorized cause), simply to avoid paying separation pay. Or an employer facing a genuine misconduct case might mistakenly process it as a redundancy, paying separation pay for an offense that legally did not require it.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Picture a small manufacturing company that eliminates a supervisory position because a new system made it redundant. Instead of processing it correctly, management tells the employee his output was substandard and dismisses him for cause. The employee investigates, realizes his position was simply removed from the organizational chart, and files a complaint. Once the labor tribunal sees through the mislabeling, the dismissal can be declared illegal. That can mean back wages and either reinstatement or, if the working relationship cannot be repaired, separation pay in its place. It is a far bigger bill than doing it correctly in the first place.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The lesson: the label an employer puts on a dismissal has to match what is actually happening. Tribunals look past the paperwork to the real reason behind the termination, and a mismatch is where employers lose cases they otherwise could have won cleanly.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Paperwork Is not the Same Either</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Just cause and authorized cause do not just differ in what is owed — they follow entirely different procedural tracks.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A just cause dismissal requires <strong>two written notices</strong>. The first notice must spell out the specific charge, the facts behind it, and the company rule or law that was violated. A vague accusation will not do. It must also give the employee a reasonable time to respond in writing, which the rules define as at least five calendar days.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In between, the employee must be given a genuine chance to be heard. This does not always mean a formal, courtroom-style hearing. A written explanation or an informal conference can be enough. But a formal hearing or conference becomes necessary when the employee asks for one in writing, when the key facts are seriously disputed, or when company rules or a union agreement require it. Only after that comes the second notice, which announces the decision and states that all the circumstances were considered and that the grounds for dismissal have been established.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">An authorized cause dismissal works differently. There is no misconduct to explain away, so instead the law requires a <strong>30-day advance written notice</strong>, stating the ground, to both the employee and the DOLE regional office before the termination takes effect. No hearing is needed for business-related grounds; the notice period itself is the safeguard, giving the employee time to look for other work and giving DOLE visibility into the closure or downsizing. Illness cases are unique: the Supreme Court requires serving two sequential notices directly to the employee (first, a notice giving them a chance to review and contest the medical finding, followed by a final decision notice), in addition to filing a 30-day advance notice with DOLE.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Happens When the Paperwork Goes Wrong</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Procedure and reason are two separate tests. If the employer has a genuine, provable reason but skips or botches the required steps, the dismissal itself usually stays valid. But the employer pays a penalty called nominal damages for ignoring the employee's right to proper process. Courts have commonly set this at around ₱30,000.00 for a just cause dismissal and ₱50,000.00 for an authorized cause dismissal. Those figures are guideposts rather than fixed price tags, and the amount can go up or down depending on the circumstances. For authorized causes, the separation pay is still owed on top of it.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The reverse is also true, and it is the bigger danger: perfect paperwork cannot rescue a dismissal that has no real reason behind it. Without a valid just or authorized cause, the dismissal is illegal no matter how neatly the notices were written.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">A Quick Gut-Check</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If you are an employer weighing a termination, ask: is this really about something the employee did, or is it about something the business needs? Be honest about which one it actually is, because the consequences of guessing wrong land squarely on the company.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If you are an employee facing dismissal, ask: was I handed a written charge with specific details and a real chance to explain myself, or was I given a 30-day notice with a separation pay computation? The answer tells you which track your employer is supposed to be following — and whether they actually followed it.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">When in doubt, on either side of the table, it is worth having the specific facts reviewed before assuming either category applies.</p>
