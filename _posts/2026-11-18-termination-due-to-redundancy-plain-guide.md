    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Is Your Job Really “Redundant”? A Plain Guide to Termination Due to Redundancy</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on November 18, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Restructuring sounds tidy on a slide deck: fewer boxes, lower costs, a leaner company. But on the day a real person receives a termination letter, “redundancy” stops being a business term and becomes a legal question. Is that position truly unnecessary, or was it simply inconvenient? Labor tribunals in the Philippines ask this question every day, and an employer who cannot prove the answer pays for it.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">Whether you are a business owner planning a reorganization or an employee holding a notice you did not expect, this guide explains, in plain terms, what redundancy is and what the law requires. The central message is simple: an employer has the right to reorganize its business, but it must be able to prove that the <em>position</em>, and not merely the employee, has genuinely become redundant.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">So, What Is Redundancy?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Redundancy is one of the reasons the Labor Code allows an employer to end employment for an “authorized cause,” meaning a reason that has nothing to do with the employee’s fault. It is found in Article 298 of the Labor Code (formerly Article 283).</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In simple terms, redundancy exists when the services of an employee are in excess of what the business reasonably needs. A position becomes redundant when it is no longer necessary to the actual requirements of the enterprise. This may happen because of overhiring, reduced business volume, the discontinuance of a product line, or changes in the way the business operates.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The key point is that redundancy is about the <em>position</em>, not the <em>person</em>. It is not a way to remove someone the company dislikes, to address poor performance, or to punish misconduct. Those situations have their own rules. And although the position must genuinely be unnecessary, the employer must also be able to explain why a particular position was abolished, or why a particular employee was selected, when comparable positions are retained or only some employees performing similar functions are affected.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">When the Work Still Exists</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A common misconception is that redundancy is valid only when the work itself disappears. That is not necessarily the case. An employer may redistribute the functions of an abolished position among existing employees, reorganize departments, or adopt a different operating structure. The fact that some of the former duties continue to be performed does not automatically invalidate redundancy.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">What matters is whether the position has genuinely become unnecessary as a separate position, and whether the employer can substantiate its business reasons. However, simply changing a job title, moving substantially identical duties to a newly created position, or hiring another person to do essentially the same work may show that the supposed redundancy was not genuine.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Should the Employer Do First?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Before any letter is written, the employer should build the foundation. A termination due to redundancy will be examined closely, and the employer carries the burden of proving that it was genuine. Sensible first steps include:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Identify the actual business reason and document it.</strong> The employer must be prepared to support the claimed redundancy with evidence, not merely a statement that the organization needs to downsize. Management approval, a new staffing structure, and a comparison of the organization before and after the change are all helpful.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Decide in advance how affected positions and employees will be determined.</strong> The method should be fair, objective, and applied evenly.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Examine future hiring and newly created positions.</strong> If the employer hires new employees or creates new positions that essentially perform the same functions as those supposedly abolished, this may undermine the claim of genuine redundancy and point to bad faith. The Supreme Court has treated such circumstances as significant evidence against the validity of a redundancy program.</li>
        </ul>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Four Legal Requirements</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">For a termination due to redundancy to stand, these four things must be present:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>1. Written notice, one month ahead.</strong> The employer must give written notice to both the affected employee and the Department of Labor and Employment (DOLE) at least one month before the termination takes effect. The employee remains employed until that date.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>2. Separation pay.</strong> The employee is entitled to at least one month’s pay, or one month’s pay for every year of service, whichever is higher. A remaining period of at least six months is counted as one full year. (Retrenchment, a different authorized cause aimed at preventing business losses, uses a different formula.)</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>3. Good faith.</strong> The position must be abolished for a genuine business reason, not as a scheme to remove a particular employee or to defeat the employee’s rights.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>4. Fair and reasonable criteria.</strong> Where the employer must decide which positions or employees will be affected, the criteria must be fair and reasonable. These may include preferred status, efficiency, seniority, and other relevant considerations, depending on the circumstances.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A word on the fourth requirement: it is not a mechanical exercise of ranking every employee in the company. The nature of the restructuring matters. When an entire department or all positions in a particular service line are abolished, and everyone holding those positions is affected, there may be no need to apply selection criteria to determine who should be dismissed. The question then centers on whether the positions were genuinely rendered unnecessary, and the employer must still prove that the abolition was genuine. However, when only some positions are abolished while comparable positions are retained, the employer must be able to explain the basis for its selection using fair and reasonable criteria.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Two Supreme Court Decisions Worth Knowing</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In <em>Aguilera v. Coca-Cola FEMSA Philippines, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 238941, September 29, 2021), the Supreme Court ruled that a redundancy was not genuine. The company abolished the employee’s position but later created another position carrying essentially the same functions, and the restructuring was not adequately supported by evidence. The lesson is that an employer cannot simply change a job title and call it redundancy when substantially the same position and functions remain. The actual organizational changes and the evidence supporting them matter.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In January 2026, the Supreme Court reached a different result in <em>Coca-Cola Europacific Aboitiz Philippines, Inc. v. Osongco, Jr., et al.</em> (G.R. Nos. 238975-76 and 239050-51, January 13, 2026), upholding the redundancy program implemented by the company. The case involved a broader restructuring of the company’s sales and distribution operations. The decision illustrates that employers may legitimately reorganize their business operations, even when doing so results in the abolition of existing positions. The employees also alleged that they continued performing substantially the same work after their separation through The Red System Company, Inc., which they described as the company’s in-house agency. The Court found no evidence that their former positions had merely been outsourced to that company. It also explained that engaging an independent contractor after a redundancy program does not, by itself, show bad faith. However, the validity of redundancy always depends on the actual circumstances and the evidence supporting the restructuring.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Read together, the two decisions send the same message. The Supreme Court looks at the real restructuring and the evidence behind it, and does not simply accept management’s description of what happened.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Mistakes That Can Undermine a Redundancy Program</h4>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Giving notice late, or forgetting to notify DOLE.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Having no evidence that a real restructuring took place.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Selecting employees arbitrarily, or targeting people the company wants to remove for other reasons.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Hiring new employees or creating positions that substantially keep the functions of the abolished positions, without a convincing explanation for the change.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Paying less separation pay than the law requires.</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The consequences depend on the kind of defect:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>If the redundancy itself is not genuine</strong> (a substantive defect), the dismissal may be declared illegal. The employee is generally entitled to reinstatement without loss of seniority rights and full backwages. When reinstatement is no longer viable or feasible, the Court may instead award separation pay in lieu of reinstatement, in addition to the appropriate backwages. Damages and attorney’s fees are awarded only when the law and the facts support them.</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>If the redundancy is valid but the notice requirements were not followed</strong> (a procedural defect), the dismissal is not automatically illegal. Under <em>Jaka Food Processing Corporation v. Pacot</em> (G.R. No. 151378, March 28, 2005), a case involving retrenchment whose reasoning covers authorized causes generally, the employer may instead be ordered to pay nominal damages. In that case, the Supreme Court upheld the dismissals and fixed the amount at ₱50,000 for each employee.</li>
        </ul>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">One more caution for employers: even when a dismissal is ultimately declared valid, an employer may still incur reinstatement-wage liability because of an earlier Labor Arbiter decision ordering reinstatement. Under Article 229 of the Labor Code, a Labor Arbiter’s decision ordering reinstatement is immediately executory, even while an appeal is pending. The employer must either actually reinstate the employee or, at its option, place the employee on the payroll. Failure to comply may result in liability for reinstatement wages even if the dismissal is ultimately declared valid, subject to the effect of any restraining order issued by the proper tribunal.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In the <em>Osongco</em> case, the Supreme Court upheld the redundancy program but still ordered the company to pay reinstatement wages from December 27, 2013, when the Labor Arbiter ordered reinstatement, until April 8, 2015, when the NLRC issued an order restraining the implementation of the writ of execution. That period covered approximately fifteen months.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">If You Are the Employee: Questions Worth Asking</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Receiving a redundancy notice is stressful, but you are entitled to understand what is happening. It is reasonable to ask:</p>
        <ul style="margin-bottom: 15px; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Does my position really cease to exist, or will another employee or a newly hired person continue performing substantially the same functions?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Did I receive a written notice at least one month before my last day, and was DOLE notified?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">What is the reason for the redundancy, and why was my position selected for abolition?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">How was my separation pay computed? Does it match my years of service?</li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;">Does my final pay include my other earned benefits, such as unpaid salary, proportionate 13th month pay, and convertible leave credits, where conversion applies under the law, company policy, or a collective bargaining agreement?</li>
        </ul>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">On timing, DOLE Labor Advisory No. 06, Series of 2020 provides that final pay, which includes separation pay, should be released within thirty (30) days from the date of separation, unless a more favorable company policy or agreement applies. The same advisory requires the employer to issue a certificate of employment within three (3) days from the employee’s request. Employers should plan for these timelines, and employees who do not receive their final pay on time may bring the matter to the nearest DOLE office.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Read any document carefully before signing it, especially a release or quitclaim. If something is unclear, ask questions first, or speak with a lawyer.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Bottom Line</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Redundancy is a lawful tool, but it is not a shortcut. For employers, the safest path is preparation: a real reason, solid evidence, fair selection, proper notice, and correct payment. For employees, the key is knowing that a “redundant” label must be backed by facts. When both sides understand the rules, restructuring can be done in a way that is lawful, fair, and respectful of the people affected.</p>
