    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Employee Tested Positive in a Random Drug Test: Can You Fire Them Right Away?</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on July 28, 2026 &bull; Revised on November 17, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Picture this: your company has put its drug-free workplace program in place and conducts a scheduled random drug test. One employee's screening result comes back positive, and HR is already drafting a termination letter. Before that letter goes out, pause. A positive screening result is serious, but it is not, by itself, a license to dismiss.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The short answer is no, dismissal is not automatic. The law allows employers to discipline employees who use dangerous drugs, even up to dismissal, but only after the result is properly confirmed, the circumstances are weighed, and the employee has been given a fair chance to answer. Skip those steps, and even a truly positive result can turn into an illegal dismissal case.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Where the Rules Come From</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Three sources work together. First, Section 36(d) of Republic Act No. 9165, the Comprehensive Dangerous Drugs Act, allows random drug testing of employees under the company's work rules, at the employer's expense, and provides that an employee found positive for the use of dangerous drugs shall be dealt with administratively, which shall be a ground for suspension or termination, subject to the Labor Code. Second, DOLE Department Order No. 53-03 requires private establishments with ten or more workers to adopt a drug abuse prevention and control program, of which random testing is one component, and explains how testing, confidentiality, and treatment should work. Third, the Labor Code still requires a just cause and due process for any dismissal.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Step One: Make Sure the Result Is Real</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A screening test is an initial test that identifies a presumptive positive result. By itself, it is not enough to establish a confirmed positive for purposes of disciplinary action. Whenever a screening result is positive, a confirmatory test, which uses a different and more specific method, must follow. Both tests must be done by a drug testing center accredited by the Department of Health (DOH). The employee must be informed of the results, whether positive or negative, and the employer bears the cost of the testing. An employer should never base disciplinary action on a screening result alone.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court drove this point home in <em>Nacague v. Sulpicio Lines, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 172589, August 8, 2010). A ship officer tested positive in a random drug test and was dismissed. The company had given him notice and even held an investigation. Still, the dismissal was struck down because only a screening test was done, and the company failed to prove that the clinic was DOH-accredited. Since the drug use was not clearly proven, the Court declared the dismissal illegal and reinstated the Labor Arbiter's award of backwages and separation pay, the latter in lieu of reinstatement because reinstatement was no longer feasible. The lesson is simple: perfect paperwork cannot rescue weak proof.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Step Two: Keep the Result Confidential</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Drug test results must be treated as confidential, and DOLE's rules say so expressly. Under the Data Privacy Act, information about a person's health is sensitive personal information. Access should be limited to the people who genuinely need to know, such as authorized HR personnel, the company's assessment team, and the company physician. DOLE's rules allow exceptions only where disclosure is required by law, where overriding public health and safety concerns exist, or where the employee concerned authorizes it in writing. Unnecessary or unauthorized disclosure may create separate data privacy exposure for the company.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Step Three: Let the Assessment Team Evaluate</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Once a positive result is confirmed, the company's Assessment Team, composed of trained members of the safety and health committee, including occupational safety and health personnel, HR managers, and employer and worker representatives, evaluates the result and determines the appropriate level of care and administrative intervention. Referral to a DOH-accredited treatment and rehabilitation center is available to employees who are diagnosed with drug dependence for the first time, who ask the team for help, or who would benefit from treatment. After rehabilitation, the team recommends whether the employee may safely return to work.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Two points are worth keeping in mind. First, a positive test is not the same as a diagnosis of drug dependence. A test only shows that a substance was detected, while dependence is a medical finding made through assessment. So a first positive result does not automatically lead to rehabilitation, and it does not automatically lead to dismissal either. Second, rehabilitation is not a free pass. An employee who keeps using drugs even after being given ample opportunity for treatment may face the penalties the law provides.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">When Dismissal Is on the Table</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The law does not limit dismissal only to drug dealing or repeat offenders. Section 36(d) makes a positive result a ground for suspension or termination, subject to the Labor Code. Read together with the confirmatory testing requirement, this means the employer must establish a properly confirmed positive result before imposing disciplinary action on the basis of the test. Depending on the circumstances, the employer may then invoke a just cause under Article 297 of the Labor Code, such as serious misconduct or willful disobedience of a reasonable and lawful company rule. The employer must establish the elements of the particular ground invoked, not merely rely on the existence of a positive test result, and the penalty must fit the offense. Relevant factors include whether the policy was clearly communicated, whether the employer obtained the employees' written acknowledgment that they had read and understood it (as DOLE requires), the employee's explanation, the nature of the job, and the employee's record.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">One more distinction is worth keeping in mind. A confirmed positive test shows that the tested substance was present. It does not, by itself, establish that the employee was impaired while working or that the substance was used unlawfully, since the law recognizes the legitimate medical use of dangerous drugs administered by a licensed practitioner. Employers should consider these circumstances and hear the employee's explanation before deciding on the appropriate disciplinary action, including how serious the offense is and whether the penalty is proportionate.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Does a First Positive Result Prevent Dismissal?</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">A first positive drug test result does not, by itself, protect an employee from dismissal. Neither does it automatically justify dismissal. The employer must consider the confirmed result, the employee's explanation, the company's policy, the nature of the work, and the evaluation of the Assessment Team. DOLE Department Order No. 53-03 does not require rehabilitation in every case. It assigns the Assessment Team the responsibility of determining whether an employee found positive needs referral for treatment or rehabilitation.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Dismissal after a first positive result may be justified, depending on the circumstances. The employer should be able to establish a properly confirmed positive result, show that the employee was covered by a clear and properly communicated drug-free workplace policy (with the employee's written acknowledgment), and prove the elements of the just cause invoked under Article 297. The nature of the employee's duties is relevant, particularly where the position involves operating heavy machinery or otherwise presents significant safety risks. The employer must also consider the employee's explanation, the Assessment Team's evaluation, the employee's record, and the proportionality of the penalty. Even where dismissal is justified, the two-notice process must still be observed.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Supreme Court's decision in <em>Villarico v. D.M. Consunji, Inc.</em> (G.R. No. 255602, August 4, 2021, affirmed with modification in a Resolution dated March 3, 2025) shows that an unrefuted positive result can support dismissal for serious misconduct. Villarico, a crane operator, underwent a medical examination that the company described as a pre-employment examination, not a random drug test. The company's records showed that he tested positive for tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), and, according to the company, a confirmatory test was also positive. The employee handbook prohibited the non-prescription use of controlled substances, and the company's Code of Conduct imposed dismissal for the use of prohibited drugs. Villarico presented no evidence to refute the findings. The Court noted that he may not have been caught using drugs, but held that his failure to disprove the positive result could not be disregarded, and it treated the use of illegal drugs as serious misconduct. The dismissal was upheld as being for just cause. However, because the company had not served the two required notices, it was ordered to pay PHP 30,000 in nominal damages.</p>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Two cautions apply. Because the employee did not challenge the testing itself, the Court did not examine accreditation or confirmatory-test compliance, and it did not address whether he was a first-time offender or whether treatment should have been offered first. The case is therefore best read as an illustration of just cause and due process. For what an employer must prove about the test itself, <em>Nacague</em> remains the better guide.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Two-Notice Process</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">When dismissal is warranted, the employer must follow procedural due process under Article 292(b). The first written notice states the specific grounds for the proposed dismissal and gives the employee at least five calendar days from receipt to submit a written explanation, as DOLE Department Order No. 147-15 provides. The employee must also be given a meaningful opportunity to be heard and to defend against the charges. After considering that explanation, the employer issues a second written notice stating its decision and the reasons for it. A formal hearing or conference is not required in every case, but it becomes mandatory when the employee requests one in writing, when substantial evidentiary disputes exist, when company rules or practice require it, or when similar circumstances justify it.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Happens If the Company Gets It Wrong</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The employer carries the burden of proving that the dismissal was for a valid cause, and Article 294 of the Labor Code protects the employee's security of tenure. If the employee successfully challenges the dismissal, the employer may be ordered to reinstate the employee and pay full backwages. If reinstatement is no longer feasible, separation pay may be awarded in its place. Additional damages may also be imposed when independently justified by the circumstances and the law.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Practical Takeaway</h4>
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Keep a written drug-free workplace policy and secure each employee's acknowledgment. Use a DOH-accredited center and insist on a confirmatory test. Guard the result's confidentiality. Let the Assessment Team evaluate the case, and consider treatment and rehabilitation as DOLE's guidelines provide. If dismissal is still warranted, prove the just cause, choose a penalty that fits, and follow the two-notice process to the letter. A drug-free workplace and a legally sound dismissal can go together, but only when each step is done by the book.</p>

Sulpicio Lines, Inc.</em>, G.R. No. 172589, August 8, 2010; <em>Villarico v. D.M. Consunji, Inc.</em>, G.R. No. 255602, August 4, 2021, as modified by the Resolution of March 3, 2025.</p>
