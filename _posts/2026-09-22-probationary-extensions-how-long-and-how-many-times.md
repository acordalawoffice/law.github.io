    <h3 style="font-size: 2.0rem; color: #1a202c; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 10px;">Probationary Extensions: How Long, and How Many Times?</h3>
    <div class="article-meta" style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: #718096; margin-bottom: 20px;">Published on September 22, 2026 &bull; Legal Educational Article</div>

    <div class="article-body" style="color: #2d3748; line-height: 1.7; text-align: justify;">
        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Our earlier piece on extending a probationary period drew a follow-up question we get often: if an extension is possible, how long can it run, and how many times can the parties agree to extend?</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">The honest answer is that the law does not give a number for either question&mdash;and understanding why is more useful than memorizing a figure that does not exist.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">No Fixed Maximum Length, and No Fixed Maximum Count</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">The Labor Code does not prescribe a fixed maximum number of months for an agreed extension, nor does it establish a specific maximum number of extensions.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">In <em>Mariwasa Manufacturing, Inc. v. Leogardo, Jr.</em> (G.R. No. 74246, January 26, 1989), the Supreme Court upheld a three-month extension agreed upon after the original six-month probationary period. The Court has also recognized, in other circumstances, longer probationary periods agreed upon by the parties from the outset, where justified by the nature of the work.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">Neither of these lines of cases translates into a formula. There is no rule that says an extension may run for three months but not four, or that a first extension is fine but a second is automatically void.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">But That Does not Mean Anything Goes</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">This does not mean an employer may repeatedly extend probation indefinitely.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An extension is an exception to the general six-month rule, not a routine tool. The employer bears the burden of showing that the extension is warranted and is not being used as a device to prevent the employee from acquiring regular status.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">That burden gets harder to carry the longer, or the more often, an employer extends. A single, targeted extension tied to a specific and identified performance gap looks like a genuine second chance. A lengthy extension, or a second and third round of extensions, starts to look like something else&mdash;an attempt to keep the employee in probationary status indefinitely, which is precisely what the security-of-tenure protections in the Labor Code are meant to prevent.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">Ask the Right Question</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">For that reason, employers should not think in terms of a "maximum number of extensions" or a "maximum number of months." The better question is whether the particular extension is:</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Legally justified</strong> &mdash; tied to a specific, identified performance gap, with a genuine reason to believe additional time will actually resolve it.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Definite</strong> &mdash; a clear start and end date, not open-ended or automatically renewable.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Voluntarily agreed upon</strong> &mdash; the employee's consent is genuine, not obtained by leaving them no real alternative.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;"><strong>Made before the employee has already acquired regular status</strong> &mdash; agreed to at or before the expiration of the then-current probationary period, never after.</p>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">An extension that satisfies all four is far more defensible than one measured against an arbitrary number of months or a headcount of prior extensions. An extension that fails any one of these&mdash;particularly the last one&mdash;is vulnerable regardless of how short it is or whether it is the first one attempted.</p>

        <h4 style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Practical Takeaway</h4>

        <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">If you are considering more than one extension, or an extension longer than the three months upheld in <em>Mariwasa</em>, treat that as a signal to slow down and document your reasoning carefully, not as something the law simply permits by default. The further a proposed extension departs from a single, short, well-justified second chance, the more it needs to be able to answer the four questions above&mdash;because that, and not a fixed number, is what a tribunal will actually be looking at.</p>
